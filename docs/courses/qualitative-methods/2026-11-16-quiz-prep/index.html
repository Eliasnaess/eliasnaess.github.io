1 Research questions, concepts and causal inference 26 Oct

McCauley & Ruggeri (2020)"From Questions and Puzzles to Research Project", SAGE Handbook ch. 2

Formulating a research question is a craft that can be practised systematically: start from the literature, simplify, move up or down a ladder of abstraction, and check the question against a few pillars.

Private vs public literature review

Private = your own map of assumptions and gaps (logic of exploration). Public = the 'storefront display' for readers (logic of explanation), citing only what matters.

Hedgehog vs fox

Know a lot about one case (why the Mau Mau rebelled) vs a little about many (why civil wars start). The trade-off is causal identification vs breadth.

Ladder of abstraction

Sartori-style steps from a specific event to a general question: Mr Green (a dictator) didn't attack Blueland in 1975 → … → drop the opponent (dyadic → monadic), keep the scope condition, pose it positively: 'Why do dictators decide to go to war?'

Four pillars (+1)

A puzzle, a gap, a real-world problem, feasibility — plus the fifth pillar, passion for the topic.

Puzzle formula

Gustafsson & Hagström: 'Why x despite y?' Zinnes: puzzles are questions, but not every question is a puzzle.

Ten question types

From 'What is Y?' (conceptual, typologies) through 'Why Y?', 'Under what conditions Y?', 'Does X cause Y?' (causes of effects) and 'What is the effect of X on Y?' (effects of causes) to conditional and temporal questions. Y-focused (explanandum) vs X-focused (explanans).

Elevator test

Can you state your question and the gist of the answer in about 45 seconds? Grandma test = clarity; policy-maker test = 'so what?'

Schwartz-Shea & Yanow (2012)Interpretive Research Design, ch. 2 "Ways of Knowing"

Interpretive research starts from a puzzle that arises when prior expectations meet lived experience, and reasons abductively, back and forth between field and literature, towards situated, meaning-focused explanation.

Topic vs question

A topic (welfare policy implementation) is broader than a question (how clients and street-level bureaucrats experienced the 1996 US welfare reform).

Deduction

Theory → hypotheses → test against observations (Zirakzadeh's original survey design).

Induction

Particular observations → general laws.

Abduction (Peirce)

Starts with a puzzle, surprise or tension and asks what would make it less surprising. Iterative–recursive, circular-spiral, explanation as situated as the puzzle.

Zirakzadeh

Planned to survey 50–100 Basque activists; the questions bored respondents and 'modernization' didn't fit, so he jettisoned the survey to capture participants' own understandings.

Lamp post

Start looking 'where the light is' (familiar literature or setting) and widen in concentric circles.

Hermeneutic circle (Dilthey, Gadamer)

No fixed starting point, no final conclusions — only stopping points; the part is understood through the whole and vice versa (a spiral).

Stranger vs familiar

Being a stranger reveals what insiders take for granted; familiarity lets you understand it. Hold both.

Concept development vs formation

Interpretive: concepts developed bottom-up from actors' ordinary language. Positivist: concepts formed a priori and operationalised. 'Concepts emerge from the field' is misleading shorthand — no one is a blank slate.

Interviews that disagree

Positivist: differing accounts make data suspect (who is lying?). Interpretive: the differences are what is analytically interesting.

Mahoney & Goertz (2006)"A Tale of Two Cultures", Political Analysis 14(3)

Qualitative and quantitative research are distinct cultures with different goals and assumptions across ten areas; seeing this prevents misunderstanding.

Causes-of-effects vs effects-of-causes

Qualitative: explain particular outcomes (the causes of WWI). Quantitative: estimate the average effect of X across a population (experimental template).

Necessary / sufficient causes

Qualitative causation is set-theoretic and logical; quantitative is correlational and probabilistic.

INUS cause (Mackie)

An Insufficient but Non-redundant part of an Unnecessary but Sufficient combination. Written as Boolean equations: Y = (A*B*c) + (A*C*D*E).

Equifinality

Several causal paths to the same outcome. Core in qualitative work with few paths (Moore's three routes to the modern world); implicit and almost infinite in a regression.

Scope

Qualitative: narrow, to avoid causal heterogeneity. Quantitative: broad, for leverage and generalization.

Case selection

Qualitative: positive cases on Y, no (0,0,0) cases — legitimate for necessary-condition claims. Quantitative: ideally random, on X.

Weighting observations

Qualitative: one misfit can sink a theory (causal-process observations). Quantitative: all observations equal; the overall fit counts.

Substantively important cases

Must be explained in qualitative work (realism must explain the end of the Cold War).

Lack of fit

Qualitative: explain the nonconforming case. Quantitative: treat it as error.

Concepts and measurement

Qualitative: concepts at the centre; error leads to concept revision (democracy subtypes). Quantitative: indicators at the centre; error modelled.

Martin (2024)"Theorization and Causality", in Cyr & Goodman, Doing Good Qualitative Research, ch. 4

Qualitative research, done as careful listening, helps (1) define questions and theory, (2) identify causal mechanisms and hypotheses, and (3) test them.

Theory (Eckstein)

A 'mental construct that orders phenomena or inquiry' — includes classificatory schemas.

Hypothesis

Derived from theory; predicts a relationship between variables; operationalised and falsifiable (power resources: unionization → generous unemployment benefits).

Causal mechanism

An account of how a factor has its effect — the inside of the 'black box' (Thelen's institutional conversion).

Soak and poke (Bates)

Inductive immersion; Bates' East African coffee farmers turned out to fear taxation, which led to a cross-national theory of state policy.

Denmark vs Britain

Danish authors saw education as strengthening society and celebrated 'small people'; British authors saw it as individual self-development and distrusted workers. Evidence: 622 British and 521 Danish texts (1700–1920), close reading, archives.

Context conditionality

Falleti & Lynch: context mediates how a mechanism works — culture as context for working-class power.

A factor can be relevant without being causal

It may set the context for the factors that drive the outcome.

2 Cross-case methods: case selection and comparison 2 Nov

Przeworski & Teune (1970)The Logic of Comparative Social Inquiry, ch. 2 "Research Designs"

Comparative research should replace the proper names of systems with variables; the most different systems design is the better route to general theory.

Most similar systems design

Pick systems as similar as possible (Scandinavia, Anglo-Saxon two-party systems); shared traits are 'controlled', differences explain. Problem: the remaining differences still overdetermine the outcome.

Most different systems design

Start from variation below the system level (individuals); assume one homogeneous population and test it step by step; bring in systemic factors only when the assumption fails. Aim: eliminate irrelevant systemic factors.

Definition of comparative research

Inquiry where more than one level of analysis is possible and units are identifiable by name at each level.

Systems differ…

…not when frequencies differ but when the patterns of relationships among variables differ (education predicts attitudes alike in Italy and the Netherlands).

Converse & Dupeux

75% of Americans vs 45% of French had a party identification, but among those who knew their father's party it was ~80% in both → the national difference moves 'to the marginals of the table'.

Seawright & Gerring (2008)"Case Selection Techniques in Case Study Research", PRQ 61(2)

When cases are chosen from a large population, purposive selection should serve two goals — representativeness and useful variation — and seven techniques do this, each with a large-N tool.

Typical

Low-residual case (on-lier); confirmatory; probes the mechanism; representative by definition.

Diverse

Two or more cases covering the full range of X, Y or X/Y; exploratory or confirmatory; minimally representative.

Extreme

Value far from the mean of one variable (Qatar, Saudi Arabia at Polity −10); exploratory; rareness is what counts.

Deviant

Far from a model's prediction (high residual); exploratory — finds new explanations; or disconfirms a deterministic claim.

Influential

Drives the estimates (leverage from the hat matrix; Cook's distance — Nepal); checks a model; not representative.

Most similar

Alike on all background variables but X1 (and Y); Mill's method of difference; large-N tool = matching (India–Singapore, Costa Rica–Benin).

Most different

Differ on everything except X1 and Y; usually seen as the weaker design.

Synecdoche

The case must stand for the population — except for influential cases.

Slater & Ziblatt (2013)"The Enduring Indispensability of the Controlled Comparison", CPS 46(10)

Controlled comparisons can deliver both internal and external validity — if they use general variables, capture representative variation and control for rival explanations.

Internal vs external validity

Internal: the hypothesis holds for the cases studied. External: it holds for cases not studied; rests on representativeness and requires out-of-sample cases.

Against the 'elective affinity'

Mahoney & Goertz and Gerring tie qualitative work to internal and quantitative to external validity; Putnam, Wilkinson and others show the reverse (statistics inside one country, qualitative comparisons for portability).

Three criteria

(1) general variables or mechanisms — reduce, not eradicate, proper names; (2) representative variation; (3) control of rival hypotheses through theory-guided case selection.

Typological representativeness

Use established typologies of mutually exclusive, exhaustive outcomes to cover the population's range (Moore, Ertman, Luebbert). For new or continuous outcomes, a 'brush-clearing' large-N first (Lieberman's nested design).

Folk Bayesian

Pick cases whose variation existing hypotheses can't explain, rather than chasing a perfect pair.

Large-N confirms, not produces

Smith's Indonesia–Iran comparison on oil and authoritarian breakdown later held in 107 countries; the regression confirmed external validity, it didn't create it.

Luebbert travels

Fascism as a reaction to urban socialists mobilising the countryside (family farms, not big estates) also explains Indonesia 1965–66 and Thailand 1976.

Grzymała-Busse (2015) · exerciseNations under God, ch. 1

Churches are most influential when they appear above politics: a history of defending the nation fuses religious and national identity, which gives moral authority, which buys quiet institutional access.

Puzzle

Influence varies across equally religious countries and persists despite majorities opposing church influence (Polish abortion law drafted by the church, 1993).

Causal chain

Church defends the nation → fusion → moral authority → institutional access (writing laws, vetting officials, running schools). Influence can't be both partisan and overt.

Design

Three most-similar pairs: Ireland–Italy, Poland–Croatia, US–Canada; plus a formal model and regressions.

Rivals ruled out

Piety, denomination, religious competition (would predict more influence in Italy and Croatia — the opposite holds), church–state regime, electoral institutions, partisan coalitions.

3 Within-case methods: process tracing 9 Nov

Beach & Pedersen (2013)Process-Tracing Methods, ch. 1–2

Process tracing is a set of tools for studying causal mechanisms in single-case designs, and it comes in three variants with different aims and inferences.

Causal mechanism

'A complex system, which produces an outcome by the interaction of a number of parts' (Glennan); parts = entities engaging in activities.

PT vs congruence

Congruence checks whether X and Y line up as predicted (Tannenwald's 'taboo talk'); PT traces the mechanism itself and is presented as a stepwise test of each part, not a narrative.

Theory-testing

Theory before fact: deduce a mechanism, operationalise its parts, test whether each is present in a case where X and Y are present. No claim about necessity or rival mechanisms.

Theory-building

Fact before theory: infer a generalizable mechanism from evidence in a case (X–Y known, mechanism unknown; or Y known, X unknown). Two inferential leaps.

Explaining-outcome

Case-centric: a minimally sufficient explanation of a puzzling outcome (WWI rather than 'war'); eclectic, includes nonsystematic parts; resembles abduction; cannot be nested in mixed designs.

Shared assumptions

Mechanismic and deterministic ontology; mechanisms present or absent; Bayesian within-case inference.

Collier (2011)"Understanding Process Tracing", PS 44(4)

Process tracing — the systematic study of diagnostic evidence — gains rigour from good description, attention to sequence, prior knowledge, and four tests defined by necessity and sufficiency.

CPOs vs DSOs

Causal-process observations: diagnostic nuggets about context or mechanism. Data-set observations: rows in a data matrix.

Description first

Take good snapshots at a series of moments before analysing change and sequence.

Straw-in-the-wind

Neither necessary nor sufficient; passing adds plausibility, failing slightly weakens. The dress bill → romantic entanglement; the dog that didn't bark → Straker took the horse.

Hoop

Necessary, not sufficient: failing eliminates, passing only keeps the hypothesis alive. Simpson had a weapon (passes); Straker's shattered skull rules out suicide.

Smoking gun

Sufficient, not necessary: passing confirms, failing doesn't eliminate. The curried mutton → the plot began in Straker's household.

Doubly decisive

Necessary and sufficient; rare — usually built by combining tests (hoop tests eliminate Simpson and Straker → the horse did it).

Method of elimination

Holmes: 'when you have eliminated the impossible…'; Mill's method of residues.

Auxiliary outcome

A side effect the mechanism should leave behind — the lame sheep Straker practised on.

Bateson (2022) · exercise"Voting for a Killer", Comparative Politics 54(2)

Ríos Montt returned to power in free elections not through coercion, the military, party inheritance or evangelicals, but by strategically embracing populism in a crisis of representation.

Method

Deductive process tracing of five hypotheses; for each: did the events happen, and did the mechanism work? Within-case comparisons over time, with other ex-authoritarians, with civilians.

Military — hoop test failed

No special relationship: many enemies in the army; rivals had equal or better ties; 2003 help came through his son.

Civil patrols — sequence & timing

No coordinated support in 1989–90; they mobilised only 2000–03 when paid, then turned on the FRG.

Authoritarian successor party — no inheritance

No cohesion, organization, clientelism or brand (7% preferred the FRG in 1998).

Evangelicals — no bloc

Elites opposed him; religion unrelated to 1999 vote intent.

Populism (Weyland)

Personalistic leader, direct unmediated support. Explains why other ex-officers lost, the odd alliance with Portillo, and the 2003 defeat (populism is episodic).

      1. Week 1 Explain the difference between induction and abduction, and use Zirakzadeh's Basque research to illustrate abduction.

        Induction moves from many particular observations to a general law. Abduction (Peirce) starts from a surprise — a clash between what the researcher expected and what she finds — and searches, back and forth between field and literature, for the conditions that would make it unsurprising; the explanation stays as situated as the puzzle. Zirakzadeh arrived with theory-derived expectations about modernization and a survey; the field surprised him (respondents were bored, ETA was more eclectic than the literature said), so he dropped the survey and theorised from participants' own understandings.

      2. Week 1 Using two of Mahoney & Goertz's ten contrasts, explain why a qualitative researcher might reject a regression showing that wealth has a small average effect on democracy.

        First, the qualitative researcher asks causes-of-effects questions — why did this country democratize — while the regression gives an effects-of-causes answer, an average across cases. Second, qualitative causation is often set-theoretic: wealth might be part of an INUS combination or a necessary condition that an additive model blurs. Third (alternative), nonconforming cases matter: the qualitative researcher would want the cases off the regression line explained rather than consigned to the error term.

        Did your answer…

      3. Week 1 Take the question 'Why did Norway not join the EU in 1994?' and move it up McCauley & Ruggeri's ladder of abstraction in three steps.

        1) Replace specifics with categories: Why did a wealthy small state reject EU membership in the post-Cold War period? 2) Drop time and place: Why do wealthy small states reject regional integration? 3) Pose it positively and keep a scope condition: Under what conditions do wealthy small states join regional organizations? Each step trades case knowledge for generality; positive phrasing is clearer than explaining an absence.

        Did your answer…

      4. Week 1 Define theory, hypothesis and causal mechanism as Martin does, with one example of each.

        Theory (Eckstein): a mental construct that orders phenomena or inquiry — e.g. power resources theory. Hypothesis: derived from theory, predicts a relationship between variables, operationalised and falsifiable — e.g. countries with higher unionization develop more generous unemployment benefits. Causal mechanism: an account of how a factor produces its effect — e.g. Thelen's conversion, where factions capture vocational programmes and turn them to new purposes.

        Did your answer…

      5. Week 2 A student wants to study why some post-communist parties survived. Explain how Slater & Ziblatt would want her to choose cases so the findings travel.

        She should (1) state the argument in general variables or mechanisms rather than proper names; (2) choose cases whose outcomes cover the population's range — typological representativeness, e.g. regenerated, marginal and vanished parties — or run a brush-clearing large-N first if no typology exists; (3) select cases that let her control for rival explanations already in the literature (folk Bayesian: variation that existing hypotheses can't explain). Out-of-sample cases, used after the argument is formed, then test its external validity.

        Did your answer…

      6. Week 2 Contrast Przeworski & Teune's most similar and most different systems designs. Which do they prefer and why?

        The most similar design picks systems sharing many traits, treats them as controlled, and explains outcomes by the remaining differences; but those differences are still many and overdetermine the outcome, and generalization is limited. The most different design starts from individuals in very different systems, assumes one homogeneous population and keeps the analysis at the individual level until systemic factors are needed — eliminating irrelevant systemic factors as it goes. They favour it because it aims at general statements free of proper names.

        Did your answer…

      7. Week 2 You have a regression of civil-war onset on 150 countries. Name the Seawright & Gerring technique you would use for each goal: (a) see whether the mechanism actually operates; (b) find a missing explanation; (c) check whether one country is driving the result.

        (a) A typical case — a low-residual on-lier where the model fits, to probe the mechanism. (b) A deviant case — a high-residual outlier the model explains poorly, to search for a new variable. (c) An influential case — one with high leverage or Cook's distance, to see if it drives the estimates.

        Did your answer…

      8. Week 2 How does Grzymała-Busse's research design rule out piety and religious competition as explanations of church influence?

        She pairs countries that are matched on religiosity, regime type and development — Ireland–Italy, Poland–Croatia, US–Canada — yet differ in church influence, so piety cannot explain the difference. The religious-markets argument predicts more influence where competition is greater (Italy, Croatia), but these have less, so the evidence runs against it. What differs is the historical role of the church in defending the nation, which produced fusion and moral authority.

      9. Week 3 Explain Collier's four tests in terms of necessity and sufficiency, with a 'Silver Blaze' example for at least two.

        Straw-in-the-wind: neither necessary nor sufficient — adds or reduces plausibility (the dress bill hints at Straker's motive). Hoop: necessary, not sufficient — failing eliminates (Straker's shattered skull rules out suicide). Smoking gun: sufficient, not necessary — passing confirms (the curried mutton places the plot in Straker's household). Doubly decisive: necessary and sufficient — confirms one hypothesis and eliminates the rest, usually built by combining tests (the horse).

        Did your answer…

      10. Week 3 Which of Beach & Pedersen's three variants fits Bateson's article, and which fits a study that explains 'the outbreak of the Rwandan genocide' as a whole? Justify.

        Bateson is closest to theory-testing: she takes existing explanations (successor party, civil-war successor, populism) and tests their predicted parts deductively in one case. A study explaining the whole Rwandan genocide is explaining-outcome process tracing: case-centric, seeking a minimally sufficient explanation that combines systematic and case-specific mechanisms. (Note that Bateson weighs several hypotheses against each other, which Beach & Pedersen say theory-testing alone rarely can.)

      11. Week 3 Walk through how Bateson rules out military support as the reason for Ríos Montt's success.

        She sets up a hoop test: if military backing mattered, he must have had a special relationship with the army. He did not — he had many enemies, was dismissed in 1973, faced coup attempts, was tutored by businessmen in 1990 and wrote to officers asking for neutrality; civilian parties had equal or better military ties. She then looks for any assistance and finds none in the 1990s; 2003 help came through his son. Finally, sequence and timing show the civil patrols mobilised only in 2000–03, when paid, and then turned against him.

      Pairs that look alike and the one-line rule for telling them apart.

      • Extreme vs deviant caseExtreme = far from the mean of ONE variable. Deviant = far from what a MODEL predicts.
      • Deviant vs influential caseDeviant = big residual (poorly explained). Influential = moves the estimates (leverage × residual); chosen to check the model, not to find new variables.
      • Most similar systems design vs most similar casesPrzeworski & Teune's MSSD is a system-level strategy they criticise; Seawright & Gerring's most-similar cases are a matched pair differing on X1. Both echo Mill's method of difference.
      • Most different systems design vs Mill's method of agreementP&T's MDSD starts from individuals and eliminates systemic factors step by step; 'different cases, same X and Y' is Mill's agreement / S&G's most-different cases.
      • Hoop vs smoking gunHoop = necessary: failing kills. Smoking gun = sufficient: passing confirms. Ask: what happens if the hypothesis FAILS?
      • Straw-in-the-wind vs hoopA straw neither confirms nor eliminates. A hoop can eliminate.
      • Induction vs abductionInduction: many particulars → general law. Abduction: a surprise → what would make it unsurprising (situated, iterative).
      • Theory-building vs explaining-outcome PTTheory-building wants a generalizable mechanism and makes no sufficiency claim. Explaining-outcome wants a minimally sufficient account of one case.
      • Process tracing vs congruenceCongruence checks whether X and Y line up as predicted; process tracing tests the parts of the mechanism between them.
      • Causes-of-effects vs effects-of-causes'Why did WWI happen?' vs 'What is the average effect of democracy on war?'
      • Internal vs external validityInternal = holds for the cases studied. External = holds out of sample. Slater & Ziblatt: controlled comparisons can do both; large-N confirms external validity, it doesn't create it.
      • INUS — whose and whatMahoney & Goertz (from Mackie). Insufficient but Non-redundant part of an Unnecessary but Sufficient combination.
      • Concept formation vs concept developmentFormation: a priori, operationalised (positivist). Development: from actors' own usage in context (interpretive).
      • Selecting on the dependent variableMahoney & Goertz: fine for necessary-condition claims. Seawright & Gerring: extreme cases are fine if background cases stay in view.
      • Bateson's testsOnly the military hypothesis gets an explicit hoop test; the civil patrols are judged by sequence and timing.
      • Puzzle vs questionEvery puzzle is a question, not every question is a puzzle (Zinnes). Puzzle = 'Why x despite y?'
