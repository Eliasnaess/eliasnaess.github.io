Delegation, Discretion, Ambiguity and Agency

Every rule has to pass through someone. Van Aken and Lewis show Chinese provinces using their autonomy to skip energy targets, Hassan shows Kenya's executive bureaucracy outliving the constitution meant to tame it, Lotta, Nieto-Morales and Peeters bring frontline workers into the study of weak institutions, Mohammed shows Ghanaian hospital staff rationing care by who looks like a "villager", and Ehrhardt shows Kano officials filling an undefined rule with local norms of belonging.

1 At a glance 30 seconds

Key questions

  1. Why do some subnational governments carry out central policy while others quietly ignore it?
  2. Why does strong executive power survive a constitution written to constrain it?
  3. How do frontline officials behave when resources are scarce and rules are vague, and who pays for it?
  4. When a rule is ambiguous, whose norms fill the gap?

Key concepts

Autonomy, capacity, interests

Regulatory autonomy gives localities room for noncompliance; low capacity and rival local economic interests decide whether they use it. — Van Aken & Lewis (2015)

Legitimating issue

A pressing national concern (here, internal security) that bureaucratic elites invoke to justify keeping their agency's size and power. — Hassan (2015)

Street-level bureaucrat (SLB)

Frontline public servant whose discretion means they shape policy rather than merely implement it. — Lipsky, via Lotta, Nieto-Morales & Peeters (2024)

Informal privatisation, policy repair, alienative commitment

Three agency patterns of SLBs in weak institutional settings: pursuing personal norms or interests, filling gaps with own resources, or retreating to job survival. — Lotta, Nieto-Morales & Peeters (2024)

Client differentiation

A coping strategy in which officials ration time and help by sorting clients into deserving and difficult types. — Mohammed (2024)

Indigeneship

Being recognised as 'native' to a state or locality, required for Federal Character quotas but never formally defined. — Ehrhardt (2017)

Formalisation of informal norms

Discretion lets local norms of belonging decide formal outcomes such as who receives a certificate. — Ehrhardt (2017)

What you get out of it

  • Delegation creates a gap between the rule and its agent; weakness often lives in that gap, not in the rule itself.
  • Autonomy is a permission, not a motive: noncompliance needs both room (autonomy) and a reason (interests, low capacity).
  • A new formal rule cannot constrain the agency that is meant to implement it, if that agency's elites have a stake in the old order (Hassan).
  • Discretion is not neutral: under scarcity and ambiguity it tends to favour insiders, natives and elites (Mohammed, Ehrhardt).

Cases and evidence

  • Energy-intensity targets, China's 11th Five-Year Plan Van Aken & Lewis (2015)
    Provincial regression plus case studies of Shaanxi, Chongqing and Guangdong; provinces with more autonomy and strong local economic interests did worse.
  • Kenya's 2010 constitution Hassan (2015)
    The Provincial Administration was scrapped and rebuilt as the National Administration with nearly the same structure, functions and personnel; 50+ interviews and archives.
  • A rural hospital under Ghana's National Health Insurance Scheme Mohammed (2024)
    Interpretative phenomenological study with field diary; 'villagers' face waiting, short consultations, extra checks and denied premium exemptions, educated elites do not.
  • Indigeneship certificates in Kano, Nigeria Ehrhardt (2017)
    Perceptions survey of 420 respondents (2006–2008) in four neighbourhoods, from the Old City to Sabon Gari, plus interviews; officials favour traditional 'natives'.

Where this fits in the course

Classes 5 and 6 asked what the state can see and reach, and what society does for or against it. This week looks inside the state: every rule passes through agents (provinces, executive bureaucracies, frontline officials) who have autonomy, interests and discretion of their own. It sets up Thursday's class on non-compliance (19 Nov), where citizens rather than officials bend the rules, and 24 Nov on informal institutions, which Ehrhardt already shows seeping into formal decisions.

3 Test your understanding

4 quick questions, then 2 to answer in your own words. Nothing is saved or sent.

  1. In Van Aken and Lewis's model, what role does regulatory autonomy play?

    Autonomy is a permission; provinces use it to disregard central targets when capacity is low or local economic interests oppose the policy.

  2. Why, according to Hassan, did Kenya's executive bureaucracy survive the 2010 constitution?

    Security served as a legitimating issue that justified rebuilding the Provincial Administration as the National Administration.

  3. Which is NOT one of the three agency patterns in Lotta, Nieto-Morales and Peeters?

    The three patterns are informal privatisation, policy repair and alienative commitment.

  4. What makes room for discrimination in Ehrhardt's Kano case?

    The undefined criterion gives local officials discretion, which they fill with ethnic and religious norms of belonging.

  5. Compare Hassan and Ehrhardt: in which case is the weak institution chosen by self-interested agents, and in which does it emerge from ambiguity?

    Hassan's bureaucrats actively resist the new constitution to protect their material benefits, so weakness is chosen. In Ehrhardt the rule itself is ambiguous and officials fill it with locally salient norms, so weakness emerges from the design. Both show agents, not rules, deciding outcomes.

  6. Is the differential treatment in Mohammed's hospital best seen as corruption, coping or policy failure?

    Mostly coping: under scarcity and high caseloads staff ration care by sorting clients, which Lotta et al. would place between alienative commitment and informal privatisation. But the effect is a policy failure, since the NHIS's goal of equal access is undermined for poor rural patients.
