Shallow Secrets Run Deep

Why Political Authority Does Not Legitimate State Secrecy

Democratic authority may include the power to classify information. That does not make the actions concealed by classification legitimate: oversight offers assurance where citizens need verification, and secrets that are shallow in form can be deep in content.

2,316 words · 10 min read

Introduction

State secrecy poses an internal tension for democratic theory. Legitimate democracies rely on an ongoing relation of accountability between citizens and the state. The difficulty in legitimating state secrecy is that citizens are barred from knowing what the state is doing and therefore cannot hold the state and its classified actions accountable. Classified information produces a knowledge deficit, which generates an accountability deficit, which in turn creates a problem of legitimacy.

Despite the challenge, it is widely recognized that democratic states cannot be fully transparent to their citizens either. Some of the state’s activities – national security, ongoing criminal investigations, sensitive diplomatic communication – would be compromised by full transparency. For the democratic state, then, secrecy is sometimes necessary. In State Secrecy and Democracy (2024), Dorota Mokrosinska offers an argument to resolve this tension. Drawing on Joseph Raz’s concept of content-independent political authority, she argues that democratic authority includes the legitimate power to classify information. On her account, secret policies can be legitimate exercises of democratic authority provided they remain shallow, functionally necessary, substantively and procedurally constrained, and subject to oversight.

This paper therefore asks: can content-independent political authority ground the democratic legitimacy of state secrecy?

In response to Mokrosinska’s argument, the thesis of this paper is that content-independent authority may well ground the state’s power to classify information, but this does not establish the legitimacy of the actions performed under that classification. I argue that classification structurally denies citizens the informational basis required to hold concealed state action to account, and that the substitutes proposed to secure accountability deliver assurance rather than the citizen-side verification Mokrosinska’s own account demands. The distinction between shallow and deep secrets does not close this gap, as shallow secrets can be shallow in form yet deep in content.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 1 establishes Mokrosinska’s account of the legitimacy of state secrecy. Section 2 distinguishes the state’s authority to classify information from the legitimacy of the concealed state action itself. Section 3 argues that neither mediated oversight nor the shallow/deep distinction closes the resulting accountability gap. I conclude that democracies may have the political authority to classify information, but the question of legitimacy remains unresolved.

Section 1: Mokrosinska’s Case for Legitimate State Secrecy

A commonly held view is that states can justify secrets when their basic interests, above all survival, are at stake. However, in “Necessary but Illegitimate” (2023), Mokrosinska shows why such necessity-based defenses fail to establish a legitimate authority to classify information. She argues necessity “escapes normative codification in both the moral and legal domains” (Mokrosinska 2023, 97), thus necessary secrets can at most be a “vindicated suspension of democratic governance,” but not a legitimate exercise of it (Mokrosinska 2023, 97).

Since necessity-based arguments cannot by themselves ground the legitimacy of state secrecy, Mokrosinska develops an alternative account in chapter 5 of State Secrecy and Democracy using a Razian content-independent account of political authority (Mokrosinska 2024, 65-70). This is the view that citizens authorize the state to make decisions and enact policies on their behalf. As Raz puts it, the validity of an authoritative decision lies “in the fact that someone in authority has said so” (Raz quoted in Mokrosinska 2024, 66). This is a relatively uncontroversial feature of democratic theory: when citizens elect representatives, they authorize them to make binding decisions. Even if a particular law passes that citizens disagree with, they remain bound by it. In other words, it is not the content of the law that makes it binding, but the authoritative source that issued it. Mokrosinska’s move is to extend this logic to the state’s power to classify information. Thus, if political authority is vested in the source – not the content – then state policies to classify information are among the legitimate powers the state can exercise (Mokrosinska 2024, 72).

Finally, Mokrosinska’s argument does not legitimate state secrecy across the board and is “constrained by the considerations at the foundation of content-independent authority” (Mokrosinska 2024, 71). These include substantive and procedural constraints aligned with core principles and values of a liberal-democratic state, with accountability mechanisms to verify these are upheld (Mokrosinska 2024, 68). Foundationally, her account uses a distinction between deep and shallow secrets. Deep secrets are “secrets the existence of which citizens are not aware” (Mokrosinska 2024, 78), and because citizens cannot contest what they do not know, deep secrets can never be legitimate. They can, however, be retrospectively vindicated (Mokrosinska 2023). Shallow secrets, by contrast, are “those of which citizens know the existence even though they are ignorant of their content” (Mokrosinska 2024, 78). Accordingly, citizens may know that a classified program is in force which ensures “the demand for accountability can be raised” (Mokrosinska 2024, 79). Taking stock, Mokrosinska’s argument is a qualified but genuine account of when state secrecy can be legitimate. The next section raises concerns that challenge this account.

Section 2: The authority to classify is not the legitimacy of what is classified

A key objection comes into view if we distinguish between two different forms of legitimacy. I argue that the state’s legitimate authority to classify information is distinct from the legitimacy of state actions done under classification. We can call the former classificatory legitimacy, and the latter operational legitimacy, which refers to the legitimacy of the concealed state actions. My claim is that classificatory legitimacy does not transfer to operational legitimacy. This distinction is structurally similar to what is familiar in just war theory as the difference between jus ad bellum, governing the conditions for waging war, and jus in bello, which governs conduct within it. I argue that Mokrosinska’s content-independent political authority account successfully establishes classificatory legitimacy, but does not adequately ground operational legitimacy, which is arguably the most pertinent one.

Furthermore, a deeper difficulty with Mokrosinska’s account is that it is at once content-independent in its grounding and content-dependent in its verification. The substantive and procedural constraints that secret policies must satisfy to have normative effect must be verified against what the state actually does. Policies and processes that contradict liberal-democratic principles and values are, on her account, invalid (Mokrosinska 2024, 68), but whether a policy contradicts those principles is a fact about its content. A constraint that is never checked against conduct constrains nothing. Mokrosinska anticipates this concern, positing that “there exist forms of accountability that do not require disclosure to the general public and direct citizen scrutiny” (Mokrosinska 2024, 75). However, as the next section argues, mediated oversight as a substitute for direct citizen scrutiny can only provide assurance to citizens that their interests are tracked, which is not the same as enabling them to determine this for themselves.

Section 3: Shallow in form, deep in content

Democratic authority remains legitimate only so long as those subject to it can hold it to account. As Mokrosinska states, “the demands for accountability are a necessary (but not sufficient) condition of the authority to resort to secrecy” (Mokrosinska 2024, 75). Her account sets the bar explicitly: “those who grant that authority are entitled to verify whether the right-holder – the state – exercises it in accordance with the reason for which it has been granted” (Mokrosinska 2024, 74). In other words, citizens are entitled to verify that the state’s exercise of secret power stays within its authorized limits. To meet this demand under conditions of secrecy, two mechanisms are offered: retrospective disclosure and oversight committees. The first, she discounts herself, conceding it offers “little opportunity to take remedial measures” (Mokrosinska 2024, 75). Indeed, from the citizen’s perspective, retrospective disclosure leans towards vindication rather than legitimation of concealed state action. Therefore, oversight is the principal mechanism tasked to meet the demands of accountability.

Even under ideal institutional design, oversight seems to fall short of what accountability demands. Accountability, as Mokrosinska writes, makes it “possible for people [citizens] to determine whether they have the authority they claim to have” (Mokrosinska 2024, 68). The citizens determine. Yet the suggested institutional oversight mechanism delivers something weaker, namely assurance. Oversight committees, when reviewing concealed state actions, “owe citizens the assurance that the restriction on information has been done for legitimate reasons” (Mokrosinska 2024, 76). The citizens receive an assurance. Crucially, an assurance is not determination. To be assured that someone has checked on your behalf is not to be in a position to check for yourself.

This holds however well the committees are designed. Mokrosinska points to safeguards –adversarial composition, opposition membership, or independent expert panels (Mokrosinska 2024, 77) – yet her own endorsement is conditional: only where these succeed in establishing the committee’s credibility does “state secrecy need not be an obstacle to control and accountability” (Mokrosinska 2024, 78). And even granting their full success, the problem is structural. Verification still occurs within a secrecy perimeter the state itself defines, so what reaches citizens is not determination of their own but a report assuring them one was made. Since democratic authority “derives from those over whom it is exercised” (Mokrosinska 2024, 67), that determination must be available to those over whom power is exercised, not merely made in their name.

To this, Mokrosinska could reply that the distinction between shallow and deep secrets keeps citizens sufficiently within the accountability loop. A shallow secret, by definition, is one whose existence citizens know, so “the demand for accountability can be raised” (Mokrosinska 2024, 79). However, I argue the distinction is formal, and in practice the two often come apart. A shallow secret grants citizens knowledge that a secret exists, but it does not reveal the depth of what it conceals. A shallow secret can therefore be shallow in form yet deep in content. Its existence can be acknowledged while its democratically contestable substance remains structurally inaccessible to the demos. Merely knowing that a program exists permits formal contestation; the harder and more important question is whether it permits meaningful contestation. If a shallow secret conceals substantial exercises of power, on what informational basis can citizens raise genuine demands for accountability? And if every specific question – with or without robust oversight mechanisms – meets a classified wall, the demand for accountability is hollow.

Consider the 2013 Snowden leaks, a case Mokrosinska briefly discusses (Mokrosinska 2024, 64). After 9/11, counter-terrorism surveillance was broadly expanded under the USA PATRIOT Act. One related program, PRISM, was disclosed by Edward Snowden in 2013, which let US intelligence agencies collect private communications data on citizens from major technology companies on a vast scale. Importantly, PRISM was a shallow secret with accountability mechanisms in place: citizens knew their government conducted surveillance under congressional authorization and FISA Court oversight (Greenwald and MacAskill 2013). Yet when its substance came to light, it revealed a far more expansive use of power than had been accounted for. Troublingly, information was concealed not only from citizens but from many of the overseers tasked with checking it. Mokrosinska recognises this, too, by granting that knowledge about a classified program can still leave citizens “insufficiently informed to authorize its particular application” (Mokrosinska 2024, 64). Yet this is precisely the issue: citizens had no way to know whether secret powers stayed within democratic limits until Snowden disclosed them post-facto. Consequently, from the citizen’s standpoint, a functioning oversight regime and a failed one are indistinguishable until the moment of disclosure – not only in PRISM’s case, but perhaps as an unavoidable feature of state secrecy itself. If citizens can learn whether the constraints held only once it is too late to contest them, accountability is not satisfied.

The verifying work that establishes legitimacy then falls, if it happens at all, to retrospective disclosure. Yet Mokrosinska herself is careful to distinguish legitimacy from mere vindication: the latter comes only after the fact and, on her own argument, cannot legitimate secret power (Mokrosinska 2023, 97). Democratic legitimacy must therefore be available prospectively – to citizens, not merely overseers. But a secret that is shallow in form yet deep in content offers citizens no such standing. Even granted the assurance of oversight, citizens cannot themselves determine, until disclosure, whether the concealed powers stayed within limits. And if that determination becomes available to them only once the secret has ceased to be a secret, then what the account delivers is not prospective legitimacy but the retrospective vindication she had ruled out, now returning through the back door.

Conclusion

This paper asked whether content-independent political authority can ground the democratic legitimacy of state secrecy. The answer is: only in part. Mokrosinska’s account succeeds in establishing what I have called classificatory legitimacy, but legitimating the power to conceal is not the same as legitimating what is concealed. Oversight conducted on the citizen’s behalf delivers assurance, not the determination her own account requires of accountability, and the shallow/deep distinction does not close the gap, since a secret may be shallow in form yet deep in content. Mokrosinska’s own conclusion is modest: where her conditions hold, the “legitimate status is not precluded” (Mokrosinska 2024, 79). But not precluded is not legitimated, and I have argued her framework does not give citizens a way to establish prospectively that any given secret clears that bar. Democracies may hold the authority to classify information; whether the actions they conceal are legitimate remains an open question.
