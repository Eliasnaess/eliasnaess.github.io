The Modern Liberal Welfare State

A Case for Liberal Neutrality

Following Joseph Heath, the welfare state is best understood as a solution to collective-action problems rather than a redistributive machine. Neutrality binds the administrative state more tightly than the legislature — illustrated by Pride flags in Norwegian schools.

4,710 words · 20 min read

Abstract: The modern liberal welfare state is commonly mistaken for a redistributive machine whose purpose is to correct inequality. Drawing on Joseph Heath’s public-economic model, this paper argues instead that its central function is to solve collective-action problems under conditions of liberal neutrality. This neutrality binds the branches of government unequally: legislatures may pursue contested ideals, but the administrative executive, where discretion remains, is disciplined by Pareto efficiency and may not endorse any contested conception of the good. Through the case of Pride-flag flying in Norwegian schools, the paper shows that liberal neutrality is not a retreat from democracy but a necessary condition of pluralism.


Introduction

Is liberalism under threat? With the rise of illiberal movements across the West on both the left and the right, it certainly seems so. As polarization widens, the commitment to pluralism that lies at the heart of liberal societies is being pressure-tested. The zeitgeist of cultural malaise is captured by the ongoing culture wars. The left charges that the state does too little: that it should redistribute more aggressively and throw its weight behind equality. The right charges that it does too much: that it has grown paternalistic and culturally partisan, using public authority to advance one side’s vision of identity and recognition. The quarrel is at once fiscal and symbolic – concerning not only how the state may tax and spend, but also what it may affirm and fly above its own institutions. Either way, the legitimacy of the modern liberal welfare state is under scrutiny from all directions.

What unites these disputes is a common confusion about what kind of institution the liberal state is and ought to be. The state is increasingly treated as a means to forward one’s own moral worldview. What is often lost in these debates and warrants a reminder is the liberal state’s principled commitment to liberal neutrality which restrains it from siding with one conception of the good over another.

This background motivates the paper’s research question: How should liberal neutrality constrain the modern liberal welfare state in the face of competing conceptions of the good?

In this paper I defend the liberal state’s commitment to neutrality for the sake of pluralism. While democratic legislatures may enact policies reflecting contested political ideals, the executive occupies a different and more constrained role. It must implement the law without thereby adopting the reasoning behind it as an operative criterion. Drawing on Joseph Heath’s public-economic model of the state, I argue that where executive discretion remains, Pareto efficiency supplies the least controversial normative criterion available to it. The executive should be responsive to democracy without being captured by it.

The paper proceeds in three steps. First, I reconstruct the development from classical to modern liberalism in order to show why liberal neutrality is constitutive of pluralism. Second, I defend Heath’s public-economic model of the welfare state as an alternative to the egalitarian model, arguing that much of what the welfare state does is better understood as collective-action problem solving rather than egalitarian redistribution. Third, using the example of Pride flagging in Norway, I examine the branches of government and identify where liberal neutrality should constrain state action.

Section 1: From Classical Liberalism to Modern Liberalism

1.1 Liberal Neutrality as a Response to Pluralism

There are values, and there are principles. Values can be weighed against one another and adjusted depending on context. For example, weighing security vs. liberty, tradition vs. innovation, economic growth vs. environmental protection, and so on. Principles are different. They set limits on what may be weighed in the first place, for example basic human rights. This section will argue that one such principle of the liberal state is liberal neutrality.

To understand why liberal neutrality became so central to the liberal state and to fully appreciate its continued importance today, I will briefly reconstruct the transition from classical liberalism to modern liberalism. This serves as an instructive reminder of the liberal state’s enduring task, which is to secure the framework conditions of cooperation for a plural population without endorsing one conception of the good among competing views.

Historically, liberal neutrality emerged as a necessary response to “the inability of Christians to resolve their doctrinal disputes” (Heath 2020, 125). In pre-liberal Europe, these disagreements manifested in recurrent and often state-supported religious conflict. In plural societies, people disagree, often deeply, about what the good life is and how it ought to be lived. In the case of medieval Europe, this played out among Catholics and Protestants who fundamentally disagreed about truth, and accordingly about the proper ordering of society (Heath 2020, 118). When these competing views were fused with state power, politics tended to get existential, violent and bloody.

Political philosophers from Hobbes to Locke understood that coercive state power could not settle disputes over the good life without turning moral disagreement into political conflict (Heath 2020, 116-125). The answer was to limit the scope of the state. Instead of the state taking sides among competing conceptions of the good, it assumed a more modest role: to secure the basic conditions of peaceful coexistence that are “neutral with respect to the particular conceptions of the good adhered to by its citizens” (Heath 2020, 118). David Hume articulated a list of three “neutral goods” the “minimal state” must provide: security, protection of property rights, and the enforcement of contracts (Heath 2020, 116-118). By narrowing the state’s role, it widened the space for pluralism. Liberal neutrality allowed citizens to genuinely disagree about religion, morality, and the good life without needing to obtain political power in order to survive. This is easily taken for granted today, but stands as one of the crowning achievements of classical liberalism.

The success of this political arrangement can hardly be understated. The state’s now-limited role “produced societies that were extremely successful, in several dimensions.” (Heath 2020, 116) It laid the intellectual grounds for the Enlightenment, facilitated the Industrial Revolution, and “somewhat inadvertently gave rise to capitalism.” (Heath 2020, 116) However, this remarkable development quickly exposed significant limits. As people flocked into cities for work in “factory slums,” massive collective-action problems appeared at a new scale: sanitation, clean water, public health, pollution, and unemployment (Heath 2020, 134). Because of classical liberalism’s commitment to the narrow list of security, property and contract, it “had practically nothing to say, and certainly no solutions to offer” to the social problems that arose (Heath 2020, 134). Beyond being woefully unhelpful, in certain cases it was “positively obstructionist” to public demands, such that proposals for initiatives like building codes or sewage systems were barred on the premise that this constituted unjust interference with individuals’ property rights (Heath 2020, 135).

1.2 From the Minimal State to the Modern Welfare State

The laissez-faire of classical liberalism was unsustainable. As Karl Marx and Frederick Engels were early to point out, the liberal state was perceived as “nothing more than an ideology concocted to advance the class interests of the bourgeoisie.” (Heath 2020, 136) Given the historical conditions of industrial capitalism and the seeming paralysis of classical liberalism, this critique had real bite. As a result, many turned to forms of socialism to solve the collective problems that classical liberalism failed to solve.

What followed was a long and intense period of ideological conflict, revolutions, and world war that cannot be covered in full here. In short, the “wars of totalitarianism” of the early twentieth century led to the resurgence of liberalism in the form we are familiar with today: the modern liberal welfare state (Heath 2020, 136-137). For the purposes of this paper, the key takeaway is that modern liberalism integrated classical liberalism’s commitment to remain neutral among competing conceptions of the good with an additional recognition of the state’s necessary role in solving collective-action problems. Modern liberalism can therefore be understood as neither a return to laissez-faire nor a compromised form of socialism. It was something in between: “a framework that was still recognizably liberal, and yet offered a remedy for many of the social problems that classical liberalism had insisted the state must ignore.” (Heath 2020, 137)

Section 2: What Does the Modern Liberal Welfare State Actually Do?

2.1 The Egalitarian Misreading of the Welfare State

The most common understanding of the modern liberal welfare state is captured by the egalitarian model. This model holds that the state’s primary role is to correct the inevitable, and morally troubling, inequalities produced by the private market through redistributive means (Heath 2020, 151). Universal healthcare, pension programs, unemployment insurance, and subsidized education, to name a few, seem to all point toward the state taking an active role in moving resources from the strong to the vulnerable. Politically, this model aligns with the left-wing moral picture: as the free market produces inequality, the state steps in to equalise and promote fairness.

I argue, following Heath’s analysis, that this is a misreading of what the modern liberal welfare state actually does (Heath 2020, 165-176). The egalitarian model as a theory faces two difficulties. First, it struggles to explain Wagner’s law of expanding state activity, “which claims that public-sector expenditure can be expected to increase as a percentage of GDP as a society becomes more affluent.” (Heath 2020, 176) The empirical record shows that across developed economies, state spending has increased as the economy has grown. For example, in Canada state spending as a share of GDP rose from less than 2% in 1900 to 45% in 1998 (Heath 2020, 149). In Norway it climbed from under 6% in 1870 to 49% in 1996 (Tanzi and Schuknecht 2000, 6). Counterintuitively, this expansion has been, in Heath’s words, “largely unaffected by the ideological complexion of the political party in power.” (Heath 2020, 176) If the welfare state were primarily an egalitarian instrument, one would expect its spending to track the electoral success of egalitarian governments, but this is not the historical pattern. In fact, state spending has continued to rise even under conservative governments openly hostile to egalitarianism, such as those of Margaret Thatcher in the United Kingdom and Ronald Reagan in the United States (Heath 2020, 176). Secondly, as the next section will elaborate, a bulk of the social-insurance programs that appear redistributive are better understood as risk-pooling arrangements that correct market failure, rather than as transfers from one class to another (Heath 2020, 163).

To sum, while the egalitarian model captures an important feature of the welfare state, it fails to explain its deeper logic. A different explanation is therefore warranted, which is given by the public-economic model.

2.2 The Public-Economic Model of the Welfare State

According to the public-economic model, the welfare state’s role should be understood to primarily solve collective-action problems that complex societies generate and private markets cannot resolve efficiently (Heath 2020, 159, 162). First, this immediately provides an account for Wagner’s law. As societies become more affluent, citizens increasingly demand goods whose provision depends on collective coordination, like infrastructure, public health, waste management, clean environments, and so on (Heath 2020, 178). The private market, left to itself, struggles to provide public goods at the right level, partly because of the free rider problem. When people can benefit without paying, private actors have little economic incentive to produce the good at the socially optimal level. Therefore, the state, “using administrative authority rather than market exchange,” fills the role as the institutional mechanism capable of more effectively providing or regulating the production of these goods at scale (Heath 2020, 159).

The reason for this is nicely explained by Coase’s transaction-cost problem (Coase 1960). Where voluntary exchange is too costly, or otherwise fails to produce mutually beneficial outcomes, the state can use its coercive power – its “monopoly of violence” in Weber’s terms – to establish cooperative arrangements that private actors, on their own, could not (Heath 2020, 160-161). Accordingly, the state has an important role to play in organizing society where the free market otherwise fails. In fact, most state interventions, among them social insurance programs, are best understood as corrections to market failure, rather than as redistributive responses to inequality. Let us look at two familiar welfare-state cases: public healthcare and pensions to illustrate this point.

On the surface, public healthcare looks redistributive and is often championed by egalitarians accordingly. Intuitively, it makes sense because resources move from the healthy to the sick; from the fortunate to the less fortunate. But Heath’s point is that this confuses the program’s surface appearance with its underlying logic (Heath 2020, 170-173). Public healthcare is more accurately understood as a response to market failure. This is because at the individual level, illness is radically uncertain. For example, an individual does not know whether they will need minor treatment next year or life-saving care costing hundreds of thousands. To be privately insured against that width of situations would require either enormous savings, which few have, or a contract that private markets supply only selectively. As Heath shows, private health insurance is subject to severe adverse selection that leads to market failure. Because those who know themselves to be high-risk are the ones who buy in, causing premiums to rise, which incentivizes the healthy to exit, and causes the pool of insured people to shrink. The state’s distinctive contribution is that universal coverage eliminates adverse selection entirely, which the voluntary market cannot do by itself (Heath 2020, 172). The state can do this because at the collective level, the uncertainty that is unmanageable for the individual becomes predictable, and so risk can be pooled across the population. What looks like redistribution is therefore, in Heath’s terms, “not a general concern about equality, but rather a response to market failure.” (Heath 2020, 173) Public healthcare works as a universal state-run insurance scheme rather than a redistributive transfer. The wish to provide it to the poor on better terms is a real feature, but “it is the Pareto improvement that motivates state involvement.” (Heath 2020, 176)

Pensions follow the same logic. At the individual level, one cannot know whether one dies at sixty-five or lives until ninety-five. Saving for old age therefore involves deep uncertainty. If someone saves too little, they will outlive their resources. If they save too much, they have needlessly restricted their life while young. This is extremely inefficient. Across a population, however, this evens out – some die early, some live long – and the state can pool that risk through a national pension scheme. In Heath’s words, it is “an insurance product, designed to protect individuals from the risk of outliving their savings” (Heath 2020, 174). The result is that those who die early end up paying for those who live longer, which is not redistribution from strong to weak, but simply the payout structure of insurance.

Much of what the welfare state does is explained by the public-economic model as efficient collective-action problem solving rather than as redistribution. Public healthcare and pensions are not exceptions, but examples of this pattern. This does not mean that many welfare-state programs have no redistributive effects – they clearly do, but those features should not be confused with their underlying rationale.

2.3 Pareto Efficiency and Liberal Neutrality

If the welfare state is not primarily in the business of redistributing outcomes, but operates as a coordinator that resolves collective-action problems on behalf of citizens, the question becomes what normative ideal it should be guided by. The answer according to Heath is Pareto efficiency. A Pareto improvement is a situation where at least one person is better off without making anyone worse off (Heath 2020, 143). For Heath, this constitutes the underlying logic of the liberal state. He argues that the welfare state’s “basic commitment is to resolving collective action problems, not promoting social welfare in the aggregate” (Heath 2020, 188).

Pareto efficiency is the most neutral normative concept that lets the liberal state justify intervention in the market without adopting a particular contested conception of the good. Using Pareto efficiency, the state is concerned with whether the background conditions of cooperation can be improved in a way that citizens, from within their own ends, have reason to accept. This is why Heath calls the Pareto principle “about as attractive and uncontroversial as a normative principle can get” (Heath 2020, 190). Because a clean water system, a functioning pension scheme, public health regulation, or basic infrastructure are all public goods that tend to improve citizens’ lives without imposing on citizens what kind of life they ought to live. This is why Pareto efficiency fits liberal neutrality. These interventions secure the shared framework within which pluralism can unfold.

An analogy may help to make this less abstract. Imagine a dance floor at an event venue. The liberal welfare state should be understood as the organizer of the event, not the choreographer dictating a common dance. Its task is to secure the space, set up the sound system, keep the floor open and, if necessary, prevent some dancers from dominating it so completely that others cannot move. Different people dance differently: some go all in, some awkwardly, some experimentally, and some do not want to dance at all. An event organizer is not there to force anyone onto the floor, and certainly not to make everyone dance the same, but create an event that everyone has the chance to join.

This is what the public-economic model explains. The state does not decide which dance is best; it maintains the conditions under which different dances can coexist. Sometimes that genuinely requires intervention, but that is justified by improving the shared dance floor, not by suggesting the superiority of one music genre over another. So, the modern welfare state may tax, regulate, insure, and provide, but its justification should remain tied to maintaining the conditions of cooperation rather than coercing citizens toward one official form of life.

Section 3. How does Liberal Neutrality Constrain the State?

3.1 Three Branches of Government and Two Levels of Executive

Until now I have written as though “the state” were a single actor. But this is misleading. Following Montesquieu’s division of powers, the modern liberal state is formally divided into three branches: the legislature, the executive, and the judiciary (Rutgers 2000, 289). This distinction matters because liberal neutrality does not bind all branches equally.

First, the legislature represents the democratic arena, and it is explicitly non-neutral. The legislative is where the diverse views of a plural society are represented, contested and translated into law. These laws reflect the will of the people, which include many competing conceptions of the good. What constrains the legislature is not Pareto efficiency and liberal neutrality, but constitutional limits. A majority may not enact laws that violate the constitutional rights of citizens. It is the role of the judiciary, operating under the rule of law, to protect the constitutional floor and establish the boundaries that democratic majorities may not cross.

Where liberal neutrality constrains the state is at the executive level. In Europe, “a distinction is made between politically appointed (i.e. usually by the legislative) and professional civil servants.” (Rutgers 2000, 298) At the top sits the political executive — elected ministers and their advisers — who translate their democratic mandate into policy direction. Beneath them, and bound to the political executive, sits the administrative executive, also known as public administration, which supplies expertise and implements policy. The political executive is democracy’s agent inside the executive branch and has democratic authority to set an agenda that can be non-neutral and non-Pareto-efficient. The administrative executive, by contrast, is bound to implement policy within the rule of law, and where there is executive discretion, it must be disciplined by liberal neutrality and the principle of Pareto efficiency (Heath 2020, 187-196).

The distinction between the two levels of the executive is important. If the political executive gives direction that reflects a contested conception of the good, the administrative executive is nevertheless bound to implement it. However, administrators must not adopt that conception of the good as their own. Public administration implements the law, not the ethical doctrine that produced it. Thus, where there is executive discretion, it should operate within liberal neutrality.

3.2 Pride, Neutrality, and Executive Discretion in Norway

The sharpest test of executive neutrality in Norway today is arguably the Pride-flag debate. In 2021 the legislature amended the 1933 flagging law, expanding municipalities’ discretion to fly other flags in connection with “an event, marking, or day of note of general interest” (Lov 4. juni 2021 nr. 56; Prop. 105 L 2020–2021). This opened space for schools and other public institutions to mark Pride at their own discretion. The Norwegian parliament itself, as a rule, does not: its presidency has held since 2019 that it will not flag on days of political marking (NRK 2019; Dagsavisen 2023). And in June 2026, the head teacher of Lundamo skole in Melhus, Ann Karin Høyland, faced pressure for declining to fly the flag, on the ground that schools “should not be a political arena” (Dokument 15:3185, 2025–2026).

As a result of this tension, a written question was put to the Minister of Education, Kari Nessa Nordtun, seeking clarification on the regulation of Pride flagging. The Minister replied that Pride month is “a good opportunity to learn about equal worth, respect and safety for all people … [and] that it is important that all children feel that they belong to the community and that it is safe for them to be themselves at school” (Dokument 15:3185, 2025–2026; my translation). Nonetheless, in line with the 2021 law, she added that “it is up to the schools and the municipalities themselves to assess whether they wish to raise the rainbow flag and mark Pride” (Dokument 15:3185, 2025–2026; my translation). The question here is not whether LGBTQ equality is good, nor whether citizens should mark Pride. It is whether liberal neutrality should constrain professional civil servants, as the administrative executive, from raising a contested symbol in the institution’s own name.

The first thing to note is that the flag admits of more than one reading. On the minimalist reading, expressed by Minister Nordtun, every citizen deserves equality before the law and equal civic standing, and none should face discrimination for their sex, gender, or sexuality. Thus, flying the flag is perfectly compatible with liberal values. On the maximalist reading, the flag carries a comprehensive doctrine making contested claims about sexuality, identity, and recognition, such that marking Pride goes beyond a civic minimum. Both readings are intelligible, which is why civil society remains genuinely divided.

Precisely because the meaning is contested, the administrative executive should not settle it. A flag flying over a public building is a powerful speech act, a kind of declaration of what the institution endorses in its own name. Where the meaning of a symbolic act is reasonably contested, public officials should refrain from siding with one reading over another. That is the function of the legislature, not of administrative discretion. A municipal council may decide that its schools shall fly the flag, and the administration must then implement it; but where the choice is left to an individual official, neutrality counsels restraint.

The point becomes obvious once we ask what else such discretion would license. Why stop at Pride? The 2021 law permits any flag for a “marking of general interest,” and any official persuaded that a cause is a good learning opportunity could as readily mark World Hijab Day for inclusion, a Palestinian or Israeli solidarity day, or a Christian heritage day for cultural continuity, each sincerely meant as welcome, yet each read by some citizens as endorsement of a contested cause. We would rightly find it troubling for a public school to endorse this rotating roster, decided official by official, not because any cause is unworthy, but because the school has no neutral ground from which to choose among them. That the Minister permits Pride flagging does not really settle the matter. Her grant of discretion is a democratic act she is entitled to make, but discretion is a permission, not a mandate, and what an administrator does with permission is governed by neutrality. So long as the legislature has not mandated a marking, officials should remain neutral. This is not from hostility to what the symbol stands for, but because the alternative is an administration that decides, cause by cause, which of its citizens’ commitments the state will be seen to share.

In sum, the issue is not whether Pride is good but the limits of administrative discretion. Citizens may celebrate it and lobby to have it marked, but the administration may not decide, on its own authority, that a contested symbol means only what the majority takes it to mean. Such restraint does not oppose Pride; it secures the very pluralism that lets Pride be expressed in the first place. That same neutrality is what protects any view that does not command the majority, theirs included.

Conclusion

This paper has defended liberal neutrality as a cornerstone of the modern liberal state. Section 1 traced its origins from the religious wars of early modern Europe, where neutrality became the necessary principle by which the state secured peaceful coexistence among citizens who disagreed about the good life. Modern liberalism inherited this concept while accepting a larger role for the state in solving the social problems classical liberalism failed to solve. In Section 2, I defended Heath’s public-economic model of the welfare state against the egalitarian model. Through examples of healthcare and pensions, I argued that much of what looks like egalitarian redistribution is in fact collective-action problem-solving. Accordingly, its guiding criterion is not equality but Pareto efficiency, the most neutral standard available to a state that does not rank its citizens’ competing conceptions of the good.

Section 3 applied this result to the machinery of government. Once we distinguish the three branches, and within the executive the political from the administrative, the question is how far neutrality extends across each. The case of Pride flagging in Norway, where the legislature has devolved the choice to executive discretion, served as the case study. I argued that neutrality binds the administrative executive, and where a symbol’s meaning is reasonably contested, a public official should not settle it on citizens’ behalf, however benevolent the intent. For the power to endorse one contested symbol is the power to endorse any, and so to let administrators choose is to let them decide, cause by cause, which of its citizens’ commitments the state will affirm, a power that should remain with the legislative, not the executive.

Liberal neutrality, then, is not a laissez-faire retreat from democracy. It is a constitutive principle of a liberal state genuinely committed to pluralism. It is the discipline that keeps any momentary majority from making its own conception of the good the de-facto voice of the state, and therefore is the condition under which a genuinely plural society can flourish.
