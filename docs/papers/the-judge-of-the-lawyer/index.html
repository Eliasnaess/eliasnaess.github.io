The Judge of the Lawyer

Integrating Kantian Conscience into Haidt's Social Intuitionist Model

Haidt's model casts reasoning as a lawyer defending our intuitions. Kantian conscience — the inner judge — restores the normativity that descriptive moral psychology leaves out.

2,680 words · 12 min read

The contemporary debate on the question concerning where our morality comes from has been divided by two main groups: sentimentalists and rationalists. Sentimentalist accounts of moral judgment hold that moral judgments are fundamentally constituted by affective responses, whereas rationalist accounts maintain that reason plays the central role in moral judgment. Over the past five decades, empirical evidence from neuroscience, psychology, and related fields has increasingly favored sentimentalist accounts, lending support to the view that moral judgment is primarily intuitive rather than a result of deliberate reflection.

Jonathan Haidt’s Social Intuitionist Model (SIM) represents one of the most influential empirical and descriptive accounts of moral judgment within the sentimentalist tradition. The model, as described in The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail, assigns primacy to intuitions in the formation of moral judgments and ascribes reason with a comparatively limited function (2001, 818-819). However, as I will argue, a weakness of such descriptive models is their lack of a robust account of moral agency that we typically associate with genuine moral judgment. Without such an account, the normative dimension of moral judgment remains underdeveloped and leaves these models susceptible to relativism-related objections. In particular, moral judgment risks being reduced to a culturally contingent expression of sentiment, insufficiently distinguishable from mere taste or preference.

In Will the Real Moral Judgment Please Stand Up?, Jeanette Kennett and Cordelia Fine offer a constructive critique of Haidt’s descriptive model. Without rejecting the empirical findings that motivate the model, they argue that Haidt underestimates the role of reason in moral judgment (2009, 88), and provide additional evidence that suggests a more substantial interplay between intuition and reason. Their critique suggests that Haidt’s overemphasis on intuition in the model fails to adequately account for moral agency and normativity that we typically associated with genuine moral judgment (2009, 85).

This paper argues that Kennett and Fine’s critique can be further strengthened by drawing on Marijana Vujošević’s non-theological interpretation of Kant’s concept of conscience as an “internal judge.” On her view, Kantian conscience should be understood not as a “felt clue or special moral sense”, but as a “kind of moral self-appraisal that triggers emotional responses.” (2014, 458). On this view, conscience plays a constitutive role in the formation of moral judgment by means of moral self-appraisal, the verdict of which exerts its own affective force (2014, 465).

I argue that integrating Kantian conscience into Haidt’s SIM helps to clarify the role of reason in moral judgment and strengthens the model against relativism-related objections. By granting reason—understood through the lens of Kantian conscience—a more constitutive role, moral judgments within SIM should be understood more than intuitive affective judgments, but as genuine moral judgments that possess a degree of normative authority.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 briefly outlines the core elements of Haidt’s Social Intuitionist Model and identifies its vulnerability to relativism-related objections. Section 3 discusses Kennett and Fine’s constructive critique of SIM and their case for a more significant role for reason in the formation of moral judgment. Section 4 introduces Kantian conscience as an internal judge that plays a role in the formation of moral judgment. Section 5 integrates this account into Haidt’s SIM, and Section 6 shows how a better account of Kantian conscience strengthens the SIM against moral relativism-related objections.

2. **Haidt’s Social Intuitionist Model and the Limited Role of Reason

The purpose of this section is to briefly sketch out core features of Jonathan Haidt’s Social Intuitionist Model of moral judgment. Haidt’s model consists of six causal links that describe how moral intuitions, moral judgments, reasoning, and social influence interact (2001, 815). Central to the model is the claim that moral intuitions play the primary role in the formation of moral judgments, while reasoning holds a comparatively limited role (2001, 817-818). Rather than functioning as an internal process in the formation of moral judgment, Haidt argues that reasoning most often serves a social role, aimed at persuading others rather than generating judgments in the first place.

Moral intuitions, on Haidt’s account, are fast and automatic responses to an eliciting situation that arise with minimal cognitive effort (2001, 818). They closely resemble what is described, by Greene and others, as ‘System 1’ processing: rapid, automatic, and emotionally charged (2014, 698). Moral judgments, in turn, are “caused by quick moral intuitions and is followed (when needed) by slow, ex post facto moral reasoning.” (2001, 817) According to the SIM, reasoning is assigned a post hoc role in the formation of moral judgment.

Still, the model’s fifth and sixth links—the “reasoned judgment” and “private reflection” links—constitute the space in which reasoning operates (2001, 819). However, Haidt characterizes moral reasoning in these contexts as largely socially strategic and references ‘The Motivated Reasoning Problem’ as cause to doubt the constitutive role of reason for moral judgment (2001, 820-822). On his view, reasoning is typically deployed to defend pre-existing intuitive judgments to others or to oneself, rather than to generate or revise moral judgments in the first place.

Nonetheless, Haidt does explicitly allow that private reasoning can, in some cases, revise moral intuitions and judgments. He suggests, however, that such cases are relatively rare and more characteristic of individuals with a strong inclination toward abstract reflection, such as “…philosophers, one of the few groups that have been found to reason well.” (2001, 819). As a result, reason is granted only a minimal and largely post-hoc role in the formation of moral judgment within the SIM.

What remains insufficiently accounted for in Haidt’s model is the distinction between mere post hoc rationalization and genuine moral self-correction. The model also does not adequately explain how and why some agents revise their moral intuitions while others do not. It also does not fully account for the distinction between moral judgments from mere expressions of preference or taste. These lacunae provide the motivation for Kennett and Fine’s challenge, which presses the SIM toward a more complex interplay between intuition and reason.

3. **Kennett and Fine’s Case for Real Moral Judgments

In Will the Real Moral Judgment Please Stand Up?, Kennett and Fine offer a critique of Haidt’s Social Intuitionist Model, arguing that it fails to “present the complexity of the relationship between automatic and controlled processes in moral judgment.” (2009, 93) They argue that Haidt’s overemphasis on automatic processes in the formation of moral judgments, if taken at face value, undermines our conception of moral agency as the capacity of agents to grasp and respond to reasons (2009, 84-85). Furthermore, they argue that much of the normativity associated with “genuine” moral judgments is lost if moral judgments are treated almost exclusively as the result of fast, intuitive processes, rather than as judgments subject to reflective endorsement and agency (2009, 85-87).

Drawing on empirical studies within the social cognitive psychology literature—such as research on bias reduction, stereotype suppression, and controlled-response tests—Kennett and Fine demonstrate that reasoning can precede judgment, reshape intuitions, and enable agents to critically distance themselves from their initial affective responses, extending the role of reason beyond what Haidt’s original model suggests (2009, 88-93). For example, one such study showed that individuals with a high capacity for self-regulatory control and a strong motivation to avoid prejudice were able to exert pre-conscious cognitive control to minimize the behavioral expression of automatically activated racial stereotypes, resulting in fewer “false gun” errors during a weapon-identification task (2009, 91-92; Amodio et al. 2008).

What these findings highlight are that there may be a more integrated role of reason in the formation of moral judgment that existing accounts leave insufficiently accounted for. While Kennett and Fine point toward this more complex interplay between intuition and reasoning, they stop short of articulating the internal mechanism through which agents self-reflect and endorse their moral judgments; in other words, the mechanism of reason. Meaningful progress toward this unresolved issue can be made by adopting Vujošević’s interpretation of Kantian conscience.

As the next section will argue, conceiving conscience as an internal judge that monitors an agent’s process of arriving at moral judgments provides a more precise way of understanding how reason operates within moral reasoning, not only as a social or post hoc intervention, but as an constitutive feature of moral reasoning altogether.

4. **Kantian Conscience as the Internal Judge

Marijana Vujošević’s non-theological interpretation of Kantian conscience offers a particularly illuminating account of the role conscience plays in moral reasoning. Her analysis can clarify how conscience might be a key missing link in the interaction between intuition and reason, especially with respect to the under-emphasized role of the “private reflection” link of Haidt’s SIM, for the formation of moral judgment. To see why this is the case, it is first necessary to clarify what conscience is—and what it is not—on Kant’s account.

To begin, Kantian conscience is not a moral feeling, a special moral sense, or the same as guilt or relief; rather “it is a kind of moral self-appraisal that triggers emotional responses.” (2014, 458) Vujošević uses the metaphor of an “internal judge” to describe the role of conscience. This judge evaluates the process by which an agent arrives at and stands by her moral judgments (maxims). As a result of this self-appraisal, the judge issues a verdict of which leads to feelings of guilt or relief (2014, 449; 453). In this way, conscience is part and parcel of the process for the formation of moral judgments, suggesting a more integrated role of reason than we get in Haidt. Conscience is further understood an inner voice or process that self-examines the sincerity, certainty, and motivational purity of an agent’s own moral judgments that assesses whether “[they] really believe that something is true rather than whether something is true or not.” (2014, 468; emphasis added) Importantly, Kantian conscience possesses motivational force: the verdicts of the inner judge elicit affective responses such as guilt, remorse, or relief that can lead to action.

In this way, reason is not merely the post-hoc responses to affective states but can itself play a causal role in moral judgment, insofar as the verdicts of conscience give rise to emotions that motivate action. This account helps illuminate dimensions of moral experience that remain underdeveloped in Haidt’s SIM and, in doing so, supports Kennett and Fine’s push for a more substantial interplay between intuition and reason. As suggested earlier, genuine moral judgments cannot be reduced to fast, automatic, intuitive responses; they require agents to be capable of critically engaging with and endorsing those responses. Vujošević interpretation of Kantian conscience makes this interplay intelligible. It explains, for instance, why agents can experience guilt even when their actions align with prevailing social norms, and why some moral judgments feel more normatively binding than others—namely, because the internal judge has issued its verdict on the agent’s moral judgment.

5. **Integrating Conscience into Haidt’s Social Intuitionist Model

This brief account of Kantian conscience allows us to return to Haidt’s SIM with a clearer sense of how reason might be meaningfully integrated into the formation of moral judgment. Although Haidt’s original formulation of the SIM assigns reason a relatively minimal, mostly post-hoc function, the model still leaves space for reasoning, particularly through the fifth and sixth link – the “reasoned judgment” link and the “private reflection” link (2001, 819). However, as I have suggested, these mechanisms remain relatively under-theorized in terms of how reason operates within them.

When understood through the lens of Kantian conscience, however, these links – particularly the sixth “private reflection” link can be made more intelligible. Haidt describes the private reflection link as “having an inner dialogue with oneself” in which an agent “may spontaneously activate a new intuition that contradicts the initial intuitive judgment.” (2001, 819). Understood through the lens of Kantian conscience, we can make sense of how this is possible: the agent’s “internal judge” evaluates not merely the intuitive content of the judgment, but the process by which it was formed and endorsed. In self-examining her judgment, the agent may find that she can or cannot sincerely stand by it, thereby eliciting an affective response that aligns her intuition with her maxims.

As Kennett and Fine point out, Haidt has later acknowledged that the “tight connection between flashes of intuition and conscious moral judgments … is not inevitable”, suggesting a more active role of reason in the model (2009, 90). Similar openness is also reflected in Haidt’s other work such as in The New Synthesis of Moral Psychology, where he emphasizes that “research on morality beyond harm and fairness is in its infancy”, remaining open to new empirical and theoretical development (2009, 1001). It is against this backdrop that Kantian conscience can be seen as a promising conceptual avenue that can be integrated into the SIM and other descriptive models.

If so, Kantian conscience can help explain how reason plays a constitutive role in the formation of moral judgments, why some intuitions persist over others, and why certain moral judgments are experienced as morally binding rather than as expressions of strong preference or taste. The latter point is especially important, as it preserves the normative weight typically associated with moral judgment. By integrating conscience into the SIM, moral judgments reveal a closer interplay between intuition and reason – by passing the verdict of the internal judge – and thus acquire a higher degree of normative weight not cannot be assigned to predominantly intuitive judgments. This can strengthen moral judgments against relativism-related objections, as the next section examines.

6. **Kantian Conscience as a Response to Relativism-Related Objections of the Social Intuitionist Model

Relativism-related objections to sentimentalist accounts of moral judgment center on the claim that such accounts lack the normativity needed to distinguish moral judgments from mere expressions of taste or preference (2015, 88-89). If moral judgments are understood solely as products of cultural conditioning, tribal consensus, or evolved emotional dispositions, then morality risks collapsing into a set of context-dependent preferences rather than being proper normative claims. As Kennett and Fine argue, this picture would fail to capture the concept of moral agency and normativity we ordinarily associate with genuine moral judgments (2009, 85).

However, by clarifying and assigning a stronger role for the “private reflection” link—understood through Kantian conscience—it would prevent moral judgments from collapsing into mere expressions of culturally conditioned intuitions and judgments. The function of the “internal judge” ensures an agent’s commitment to and sincerity of their moral judgments. So even if, as the SIM suggests, intuitions are predominantly culturally and socially shaped, the function of conscience as a self-appraisal mechanism nevertheless grounds normativity in the moral judgments. Thus conceived, moral judgments express not only how agents intuitively feel, but to the extent they can also endorse those feelings.

To take stock, by incorporating Kantian conscience into Haidt’s sentimentalist account of moral judgment, moral disagreement is not as easily reduced to a matter of subjective or cultural difference of sentiment. Counter to claims of moral relativism is that moral judgments are partly intuitive and partly the result of an integral mechanism of moral agents that are committed to sincerity and consistency in the process and formation of maxims, thereby preserving a level of normativity in moral judgments. Thus conceived, differences in moral judgment arise from both divergent intuitions but also differences in how agents reflect upon and take responsibility for their judgments.

7. **Conclusion

This paper has argued that Kantian conscience can supplement Haidt’s Social Intuitionist Model by clarifying and strengthening the role of reason in moral judgment. After outlining Haidt’s SIM and its strong emphasis on intuition, the paper examined Kennett and Fine’s critique, which highlights the need for a more substantial interplay of intuition and reason. Drawing on Vujošević’s interpretation of Kantian conscience, the paper then showed how conscience can be understood and integrated into the SIM to better account for a more constitutive role of reason in the formation of moral judgment. By integrating Kantian conscience into descriptive models like the SIM, moral judgments preserve an element of normativity that is not easily dismissed by relativism-related objections. With further research on conscience in moral psychology and meta-ethics, such integration offers a promising way to bridge descriptive and normative dimensions of moral judgment.
