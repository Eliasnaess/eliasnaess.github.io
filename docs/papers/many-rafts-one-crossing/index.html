The Self/No-Self Debate · Leiden University · June 2026

Many Rafts, One Crossing

A Case for Soteriological Convergence in the Indian Self/No-Self Debate

The Upaniṣadic self and the Buddhist no-self genuinely disagree at the level of doctrine. Read through the two truths, they may converge where it matters most: on the path to liberation.

4,232 words · 18 min read

Introduction

Some twenty-five centuries ago, a man named Siddhārtha Gautama sat down beneath a bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya and resolved not to rise until he had understood the nature of human suffering. When he stood again, Siddhārtha Gautama — afterwards the Buddha, “the awakened one” — claimed to have seen through an illusion so persistent that most of us take it for granted: the illusion of the self. From carefully observing his own experience as it presented itself — thoughts, sensations, and the entire panoply of the inner theatre of the mind — he reported that he could not find a self that all of it was supposedly happening to. There was no thinker behind the thoughts, no experiencer behind the experience, no sufferer behind the suffering. Instead what he saw was a process. The “I,” the Buddha taught, is nothing but a story the mind creates. The tradition calls it papañca, mental proliferation: a running commentary that reactively produces and re-produces, ad infinitum, a narrative with a protagonist at its centre – “I”. Seeing through this process is the beginning of liberation and is comparable to waking up from a dream one had mistaken for base reality. From this seed grew the whole of Buddhism, distilled in the Four Noble Truths: there is suffering; suffering arises from craving; there is a way beyond craving; and that way is the Noble Eightfold Path.

These teaching sent shockwaves across the Indian subcontinent. The Brahminical tradition that preceded the Buddha, whose summit is the collection of texts called the Upaniṣads, had arrived at the opposite conclusion: that the innermost self — the ātman — is not only real but is ultimately identical with brahman, the ground and source of everything. On this view, the deepest fact that can be realized is not that you are empty but that you are, at bottom, the universe knowing itself. The two camps understood each other perfectly, and disliked what they understood. Defenders of this Self (hereafter Atmanists) thought of the Buddha’s “no-self” (anattā) as nihilism. No-self would mean the destruction of the very thing the spiritual path exists to liberate. If there is no self, who then is liberated? Followers of buddhism returned the compliment, and argued an eternal self is not the cure for clinging but a manifestation of its most subtle and pervasive form.

Sharp lines were drawn on both sides, and ever since, arguments have been traded like moves in a theoretically drawn endgame, neither side willing to surrender its attempt to checkmate the other. What followed was well over a thousand years of brilliant, technical, highly sophisticated debate. And yet, with over two thousand years of retrospection, if we were to take tally, the honest verdict is an epic stalemate. As in chess, when the pieces have shuffled long enough without making progress, the game is officially declared a draw. A scholar will find this unsatisfactory. The sage, on the other hand, may not. The Buddha, on one famous occasion, was asked point-blank whether the self exists, to which he simply remained silent (Saṃyutta Nikāya 44.10, trans. Bodhi 2000). The Daoists proverb captures the same wisdom: those who know do not speak; those who speak do not know. Or as Wittgenstein remarked as he closed his Tractatus: “whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent” (Wittgenstein 1922, prop. 7).

This being said, this is not a quietist paper. A drawn game is still an informative, and over two and a half thousand years of argument permit inferences, and conclusions can be drawn. This paper therefore asks: to what extent can a perennialist reading of the classical Indian self and no-self traditions yield a defensible convergence thesis, without denying the schools’ real doctrinal differences?

My thesis is that it can, but only structurally and soteriologically, not metaphysically. I defend a qualified, soteriological perennialism. The classical schools make different and mutually exclusive claims about what ultimately exists, and I will do not dismiss those differences. Yet they converge on the demand that matters most in every one of them, which is that the conceptual self must not be mistaken for the deeper insight that lies behind it. By looking at what the traditions actually instruct their practitioners to do, I argue that they are structurally the same enterprise, pursued through different grammars.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section I asks what perennialism is, which version fails, and which can be defended. Section II establishes the experiential datum the traditions share, and section III draws the doctrinal lines in the sand. Section IV argues that language itself forces those lines, and that the traditions’ own conceptual device — the idea of two truths — shows where the disagreement starts. Section V synthesizes the claim that across the schools, the methods point in the same direction. The conclusion is that there are many rafts, all with the goal of crossing the river.


I. What Perennialism Can and Cannot Claim

Perennialism is a loaded term, so it is important to define it before using it. In its more popular form – associated with Aldous Huxley in The Perennial Philosophy (1945), and that Alan Watts later carried into thousands of living rooms – perennialism is the comfortable and soothing idea that all the great traditions are, at their core, saying the same thing. For the purpose of this paper, this can be labeled unqualified perennialism. It is unqualified because it unites and harmonizes at the price of taking the traditions’ claims seriously – in a word, it is unrigorous. Besides, it can also be argued it introduces a form of meta-philosophical imperialism that the schools themselves would not accept. The fact is that the Advaitin’s Self (ātman) and the buddhist’s non-self (anattā) are not different words for the same thing. On a metaphysical reading, they contradict each other, a point the schools themselves have spent the last thousand years to underscore. To proclaim that they somehow, contrary to their own positions, secretly agree is to fail to engage properly with their premises and claims. Thus, a perennialist reading must accept that there are doctrinal differences that cannot easily be diluted.

This paper argues for a qualified version of perennialism that claims across schools of Indian philosophy convergence not in doctrine but of direction. The traditions diagnose the same root problem, issue the same practical demand, and instruct their practitioners toward the same kind of seeing, all the while describing the destination in incompatible and sometimes contradicting vocabularies. It can be understood as a soteriological perennialism: a claim about the structure of liberation, not about what ultimately exists. I argue that the most advanced and clear-minded instructions of the various traditions point the seeker in the same direction, that is, toward liberation from samsara and the unessesary and toilsome suffering of wordly existence.

What motivates such a reading? Listen to a modern conversation across the lines. Sam Harris, a secular meditator trained in Buddhist lineages that deny the self, has recorded long dialogues with Swami Sarvapriyananda of the Vedanta Society of New York, a monk of the school that affirms the Self as the only reality (Harris and Sarvapriyananda, Waking Up podcast dialogues 2023). Doctrinally, the two should be at each other’s throats. Harris denies the Self any metaphysical reality while Swami Sarvapriyananda has devoted his life to it. Yet when the conversation turns to practice, namely what to do with one’s attention, what dissolves and is revealed when the mind quiets, and what is found when one looks for the looker, they seem to understand each other almost without friction – certainly not the level of friction demonstrated in the scholastic literature. That two contemplatives across this divide mutually recognize each other as having arrived somewhere real is a datum worth taking seriously. Steven Katz famously objected that there are no unmediated experiences (Katz 1978); I therefore rest my argument not on private mystical states, which cannot be compared, but on the traditions’ public instructions, which can.

II. The Shared Datum: Looking for the Looker

Let us begin with something every school accepts, since what comes after divides them. Turn attention upon itself and try to observe the observer. It cannot be done. In the Upaniṣads, the sage Yājñavalkya gives this (non)phenomenon its oldest canonical form: “you cannot see the seer of seeing”. (Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 3.4.2, trans. Olivelle 1996). He instructs a challenger that you cannot hear the hearer of hearing, or think the thinker of thinking. His method follows directly from this. In search of the self one can proceed only through neti neti : “not this, not this.” (Bṛhadāraṇyaka 4.4.22) Whatever you can observe — thoughts, feelings, body, breath — is, for that very reason, not the observer. What is an object of experience, is not that which experiences.

The Buddha set up the same experiment by cataloguing everything that a person consists of which he sorted into five aggregates — the khandhas: body, feeling, perception, mental constructions, consciousness (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, SN 22.59). Upon examining each carefully, one comes to realize that each is impermanent. As each khanda arises, it just the same vanishes. None survives scrutiny as a candidate for “me.” Not unlike Yājñavalkya, the instruction proceeds by negation: n’etaṃ mama, n’eso’ham asmi, na me so attā – “this is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self.” (Saṃyutta Nikāya 35.71, trans. Bodhi 2000). In modern discourse, it is a truism to say that one is not identified with one’s thoughts. The Buddha, over two millenia ago, took this to its logical end-point and infered that when the search is complete, there is no-thing to find or hold unto.

Anyone can test this experiment. For example, if one dedicates ten days at a Vipassanā retreat, one’s attention is trained to sweep the body sensation by sensation until, eventually, it is turned on the one supposedly doing the sweeping, and one finds nothing to land on. Harris, coming from a Dzogchen perspective, suggests that you look for the thinker of your thoughts (Harris 2014). The purpose of these instructions is to demonstrate for the practicioner that there is nothing to find – and that is precisely the point. The search comes back empty-handed, today as it did yesterday; the not-finding is the finding. From this we can identify the shared datum the traditions share. The seer cannot be seen, because it is not one more object in the field of awareness. Consciousness, awareness, emptiness – whichever register one chooses – is that by which objects are experienced at all. The fight begins only afterward, when the schools attempt to say, in language, what the not-finding means, and what can be deduced from it.

III. The Inevitable Lines in the Sand

Having established this shared starting point, this section will briefly sketch out some of the schools and where they land. To start, in the Upaniṣads, and later for Śaṅkara’s school of Advaita Vedanta, “non-duality”, the unfindable witness is the whole point. The seer cannot be seen because it is the one doing the seeing. Accordingly, the subject can never appear as an object, and is more real than anything that does. This witness is the ātman, and the ātman is brahman (BU 1.4.10). Liberation lies in the recognition of this unity. Śaṅkara terms the fundamental error as adhyāsa, superimposition, which is to mistake that which is witnessed from the witness itself. A famous example is how the mind mistakes a coiled-up rope for a snake in a dark room. Likewise, we mistake the conventional self from the true self.

Sāṃkhya, the oldest systematic school, is dualistic and refuses the One. That which luminates is called puruṣa, pure consciousness, and is distinct from everything illuminated, which belongs to prakṛti, nature. Interestingly, in the Sāṃkhya system, the mind, the intellect, and the “I-maker,” ahaṃkāra, all fall on the side of nature. The thing in you that says “I” is not the seer, but part of what is seen (Sāṃkhyakārikā 17–19, trans. Virupakshananda 2022). Accordingly, where the Advaita Vedanta school finds one Self, Sāṃkhya argues for many selves, each being a separate and individual witness, illuminated by purusa (SK 18).

Buddhism, for its part, denies that anything stands behind the five aggregates at all. A person is not an owner above or apart from experience, but a causal process temporarily held together by craving, ignorance, and habit. The Buddha draws the boundary with deliberate finality in the discourse on “the all”:

“And what, bhikkhus, is the all? The eye and forms, the ear and sounds, the nose and odours, the tongue and tastes, the body and tactile objects, the mind and mental phenomena. This is called the all. If anyone, bhikkhus, should speak thus: ‘Having rejected this all, I shall make known another all,’ that would be a mere empty boast on his part.” (Bhikkhu Bodhi, The Connected Discourses of the Buddha,
SN 35:23)

Notice the polemical jab against the Brahminical schools. Whatever cannot be located within the six sense spheres and their objects, like an unseen seer included, is but “an empty boast.” A millennium later, from the Yogācāra tradition, Vasubandhu gave the buddhism its most sophisticated scholastic form. Defending continuity from a no-self position, Vasubandhu argues that persons are real in the same way a river is real: conventionally, as a causally connected stream, but not as an enduring substance over and above that stream (Duerlinger 2003). On this view, continuity does the explanatory work that an eternal Self was supposed to do, without requiring such a Self. Just as a flame appears to “move” down a wick only through a succession of momentary flames, a person “transmigrates” only through causal succession, with nothing that literally transmigrates.

Other schools drew their own lines. Nyāya, the tradition of the logicians, held the view that the self can never be perceived but argued that it could be proven through logical inference. For anything to have e.g. desire, memory, and recognition, it would need a subject that tie experiences together, therefore a self must be inferred behind the flow (Nyāyasūtra 1.1.10; Chakrabarti 1999). As for the Jains, they characteristically refused to absolutize any position at all, adopting a doctrine of anekāntavāda, “many-sidedness,” which held that every metaphysical claim is true from some standpoint and false from others, positing that each school grasps the elephant by a different limb.

As this brief survey outlines, these are not perspectives waiting to be synthesized, but rather mutually formulated refutations of each other. Defenders of a Self called the Buddhists nihilists who destroy the very subject liberation is for and Śaṅkara pressed the point with a devastating question: “Tell me to whom final release as the result belongs, if everything perishes” (Upadeśasāhasrī, trans. Mayeda 1992, 152–153). Buddhists responded that the eternal Self is attachment dressed up in Sunday clothes. Sāṃkhya’s host of lights and Advaita’s one Self cannot both be true at the same time without violating the principle of non-contradiction. Finally, Nyāya’s inferred soul is precicely what Vasubandhu’s ninth chapter sets out to refute (Duerlinger 2003). Taking stock, then, it is hard to deny the metaphysically irrenconsilable differences presented by the various schools.

IV. Two Truths and the Raft

The preceding section prompts us to ask: why do the schools fall, one after another, into the trap of drawing undrawable lines? One explanation is because teaching requires language, and language is built by and for the conventional world of concepts and objects. There is no grammatical way to speak from nowhere. The moment the non-conceptual is spoken of, a noun is invoked — “the Self,” “awareness,” “emptiness,” “the stream,” “Source,” etc. — and the grammar of the noun smuggles in exactly the metaphysical thinghood the introspective insight is meant to dissolve. The early discourses are remarkably self-aware about this. In one short text, an enlightened monk is challenged for saying “I speak,” to which the Buddha replies that one who is freed may go on using the world’s expressions, skillfully, without being deceived by them (SN 1.25, trans. Bodhi 2000). Indeed, even an arahant – a liberated one – says “I.” The alternative is silence, and as the wanderer Vacchagotta discovered, is sometimes exactly what the Buddha chose (SN 44.10, trans. Bodhi 2000). Indeed, many enlightened sages throughout history made a vow to remain silent. But a teaching that remains forever in silence cannot be preserved for long. Thus, as the moment the Buddha spoke, his words inevitably molded into doctrine. And doctrine means commitments, and commitments mean borders, for better and worse.

The Buddhist scholastics turned this predicament into a precision instrument: the doctrine of two truths. At the conventional level (saṃvṛti), persons, pots, and rivers are perfectly real, whereas at the ultimate level (paramārtha), a thing is real only if it survives analysis, which the person does not (Abhidharmakośa 6.4, trans. Duerlinger 2003). Strikingly, Advaita Vedānta, Buddhism’s great rival, built the same two-level device under a different name: the transactional standpoint (vyāvahārika) and the absolute (pāramārthika) (Mayeda 1992). From this can be argued that the schools’ contradictions live at the level of stated doctrine, yet every school would say agree that attachment to doctrine is not the goal, liberation is.

The Buddha’s own image for this is beautiful. The teaching, he says, is like a raft a traveller lashes together to cross a dangerous river; once across, only a fool hoists the raft onto his head and carries it onward — the teaching is for crossing over, not for holding onto (Majjhima Nikāya 22, trans. Ñāṇamoli and Bodhi 1995). Edward Bastian’s 1977 film of Sera Monastery shows what the raft looks like at the institutional scale. It shows the Gelugpa curriculum running by a precise sequence: thos, bsam, sgom: learning, contemplating, meditating. (Bastian 1977) The steps are laid out chronologically and one only graduates once each stage has been completed. First, the monk memorizes thousands of texts, then spends years debating them in the monastic courtyard, hands clapping, exhausting positions one by one. Only upon maturity, when all conceptual arguments are exhausted, will he finally enter the stage of meditation. The strict doctrine and procedures of Sera Monestary can be found in similar instantiations across the various traditions. Importantly, however, is that the doctrine is not the destination; it is scaffolding. Rigorous, demanding, indispensable scaffolding built so that that which cannot ultimately be stated can at least be reliably approached. As the schools and monastic orders keep the teachings alive, it seems unavoidable that the raft becomes a locus of attention. After all, when crossing the sea of samsara, one is well advised to travel on a sturdy ship.

Again, if we continue with the analogy of the raft, then the different schools will argue that their raft is the one that will succesfully cross the river. Each school will insist that its raft is the one that actually completes the crossing, while the rivals’ rafts are riddled with holes, poorly engineered, built of rotten wood. Remember, the stakes are enormous. If, for example Vasubandhu is right, liberation requires extinguishing the flame of attachment in every form, including attachment to a Self. Thus, the Sāṃkhya adept who holds fast to puruṣa as an eternal, unchanging witness is thereby gripping the subtlest fetter of all, and will never cross. If the Advaitin is right — if ātman really is brahman — then the Buddhist stands in a nihilism from which the saving recognition can never come, and must moreover explain how, on a view where everything changes from moment to moment (anicca), karma, rebirth, memory, responsibility, and agency survive scrutiny at all. The disagreement, in other words, is not merely descriptive but prescriptive: each tradition make claims not only about metaphysics but that the others’ method fail.

Nonetheless, a curious phenomenon brings the convergence thesis into view, which is that both of these rival traditions are in the business of guiding practitioners — and reliably produce realized human beings.Advaita with its sages and gurus, and Buddhism with its arahants and bodhisattvas. Transformation is recognizably and strikingly similar from the outside. There is in most cases an unmistakable equanimity, a selflessness and a compassion that results of someone no longer stuck in samsara. While the grammars contradict at the conceptual level, realization and its associated characteristics seem to converge at the ethical level.

V. Pointing in the Same Direction

If the bewitchment of language leads us to disagree at the conventional level, what if we stop reading the schools’ doctrines for a moment and instead turn to their instructions? Advaita drills its students in the discrimination of seer from seen: whatever is witnessed is not the witness; whatever appears in awareness is not awareness. Zen buddhism is famous for handing its students kōans, which are small questions designed to jam the conceptual machinery to stimulate non-conceptual insight. What is the sound of one hand clapping? Does dog have Buddha-nature? Answer: Mu (not) (Watts 1957) Dzogchen masters give “pointing-out” instructions, directing attention upon itself. These different schools, while using different words, they make a similar demand: concentrate the mind, pay attention, and observe as the constructed self is caught in the act of constructing.

The buddhist tradition calls it papañca, proliferation, as the mind’s compulsive self-narrating from bare experience. Śaṅkara calls it adhyāsa, the superimposition of a self where there is none. Sāṃkhya names the mechanism ahaṃkāra, the I-maker, a piece of nature impersonating the witness (Sāṃkhyakārikā 24). These three concepts are from metaphysically incompatible schools, but they point to the same fundamental mistake. The constructed, narrating “I” is being conflated with the ultimate. Where they converge, then, is in the demand made of the student: see through it. What remains after the seeing-through is where they part ways, but this should not take away the initial demand. If one were to trace the disagreements on a map, every path would still leave from the same gate.

Conclusion

Scholars will go on throwing jabs at one another; that is their work, and the debate is sharper for it. But there is also something to learn here from the Jains, and from Chakravarthi Ram-Prasad’s deliberately non-violent way of reading rival schools (Ram-Prasad 2007). Given the complexity of the Self/No-Self debate — covering consciousness, selfhood, suffering, liberation, and more — it may be too vast to be exhausted by any single system. Perhaps it is wise to think of the debate as a prism. It has many faces, many angles, and many ways in. If one face admits you, enter there, and liberate yourself. If a neighbour enters by another face, it would be a mistake to declare that her alleviation of suffering is “wrong.” The goal, at the end of the day, is the alleviation of suffering. Therefore, it may well be argued that we need many rafts, built of different materials, by different hands.

In that sense any sincere tradition may be worth its salt. What the places like the Sera debate courtyard offers, and what two thousand years of refined arguments provide is robustness, and a tested scaffolding that holds a person’s weight through the parts of the path where intuition reliably fails. It seems that no one doctrine cannot have the final word on the matter. But a good tradition can build a structure that lets the final thing be shown. And then, having shown it, has the grace to fall away, the way a raft is left at the shore by the one who no longer needs it.
