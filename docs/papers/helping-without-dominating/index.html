Helping Without Dominating

Republican Freedom and the Ethics of Aid

Poverty relief should be secured as a robust entitlement, not as discretionary help that leaves the poor exposed to donors' shifting priorities. Read through Pettit's freedom as non-domination, a Global Minimum Income can reduce poverty without reproducing domination — if its institutions are designed for it.

4,209 words · 18 min read

Introduction

In a volatile and insecure international world order, many developed countries are spending more on security and less on development aid. Aid is also increasingly being used as an instrument for diplomatic and strategic influence. At the same time, the aid debate is discussing reform, with an emphasis on transparency, efficiency, and measurable results (Civita, Note 49).

Irrespective of this, a basic fact remains: in 2025, more than 800 million – roughly one in ten people – live in extreme poverty, below the World Bank’s international poverty line of $3 per day (UN SDG Report 2025). A central concern of global justice is the eradication of this level of destitution. Yet as long as poverty relief is contingent on shifting donor priorities across electoral cycles and geopolitical circumstances, the poor remain exposed to discretionary decisions and strategic conditionality. Genuine global justice therefore requires more than reformed aid structures; it requires poverty reduction secured on terms that are robust, predictable, and non-arbitrary.

One prominent proposal within this debate is the introduction of a Global Minimum Income. Thomas Wells defends this proposal in Just End Poverty Now, arguing that an unconditional minimum cash transfer to the world’s poorest is the “least we should do,” given the moral urgency of ending extreme poverty (Wells 2019, 2). The idea is straightforward: by giving cash directly to the world’s poorest, extreme poverty can be reduced immediately, while avoiding much of the administrative complexity and mixed results associated with traditional development assistance.

Drawing on Philip Pettit and other neo-republican accounts of freedom as non-domination, this paper evaluates whether a Global Minimum Income is a legitimate response to reduce global poverty (Pettit 2006). From a republican view, poverty reduction cannot be assessed solely in terms of welfare outcomes. What matters is not only who gets what, but on what terms they get it. Domination, in the context of development, consists in a developing country being vulnerable to a developed country’s or agency’s arbitrary capacity to interfere – including through the withdrawal or conditional provision of aid.

This paper therefore asks: How can a Global Minimum Income be implemented to reduce poverty without reproducing domination?

I argue that a Global Minimum Income is consistent with republican freedom as a response to extreme poverty, and that Wells’s proposal can be strengthened by making explicit the institutional conditions required for non-domination. This matters because the moral urgency of poverty alleviation should not generate new forms of dependence and domination. The least dominating pathway is one that secures poverty relief as a robust entitlement rather than as discretionary and politically contingent assistance.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 1 develops the republican ideal of freedom as non-domination and contextualizes it within global justice. Section 2 reconstructs Wells’s case for a Global Minimum Income and evaluates it from a republican lens. Section 3 articulates some core republican concerns about a Global Minimum Income, highlighting that domination may re-enter through different forms. Section 4 compares two plausible implementation pathways for a Global Minimum Income and specifies how each can be structured to reduce poverty without reproducing domination.

1. Republican Freedom as Non-Domination in the Context of Global Justice and Extreme Poverty

To understand why a republican conception of freedom is relevant for debates within global justice, it is first necessary to clarify what is meant by freedom as non-domination. On Pettit’s account, freedom is not merely to enjoy non-interference in one’s choices (the liberal account), but to occupy a social position in which no other agent holds an uncontrolled or arbitrary capacity to interfere in one’s affairs (Pettit 2006, 224). The central concern is therefore not interference as such, but the structural relations of power that make interference possible. A person may enjoy a wide range of benefits and liberties and yet remain fundamentally unfree if those benefits depend on another’s discretion rather than on secure and enforceable rights.

In an interview, Pettit illustrated this idea using Henrik Ibsen’s A Doll’s House. (UCTV, Freedom with Philip Pettit. 23:27) In the play, Nora appears to act freely, while her husband, Torvald, rarely interferes with her daily activities. From a liberal view, Nora arguably enjoys freedom. Yet in the republican sense, Nora is not free, because her ability to act is dependent on Torvald’s permission. He is a benevolent master, but a master nonetheless (Ibid.). What renders the relationship dominating is not interference per se, but the structural power imbalance between the two. Torvald has the ability to interfere at will (even if he chooses not to), and Nora must shape her behavior with this possibility in mind.

This point is sharpened by Christian List’s emphasis on the rule-of-law-like character of republican freedom (List 2005). Republican freedom requires that protection from interference does not rest on another’s arbitrary goodwill in any one given world, but on rights and rules that are robust, transparent, and foreseeable “across all relevant possible worlds.” (List 2005, 12-13) For power to be non-arbitrary, it must be exercised according to publicly known and contestable rules. When benefits depend on discretion and goodwill rather than proper entitlements, recipients stand in a position analogous to Nora’s. They may contingently enjoy favorable conditions, but only as long as those with power do not act otherwise. These features of freedom as non-domination are particularly relevant in the context of global justice.

Although republicanism began as a theory of freedom within the state, its core concern – being subject to the arbitrary power of another actor – extends beyond national borders (Laborde and Ronzoni 2015, 279). Following neo-republicans like Frank Lovett, Cécile Laborde and Miriam Ronzoni, this paper assumes that domination is a relevant category wherever agents or institutions hold the capacity to interfere on terms those affected cannot effectively contest, regardless of territorial boundaries (Lovett 2016; Laborde and Ronzoni 2015).

Taking this view, we can shift our focus to extreme destitution and its connection to domination. Lovett states that “the extraordinary level of global inequality and poverty is a significant source of domination around the world” and that “being dependent on a person or group who has the power to arbitrarily withhold goods or services needed to meet one’s basic needs… amounts to domination.” (Lovett 2016, 41; Lovett 2010, 195) The republican logic is straightforward: if destitution renders individuals and communities acutely dependent on access to basic necessities, then they are exposed to the arbitrary interference of whoever controls those necessities. Even where access to survival goods are provided by the benevolent assistance of others, this would still amount to recipients being in a dominated position.

Put simply, if poverty is a central enabler of domination, then republicanism – beyond any other moral justification – has intrinsic reasons to reduce global poverty. This motivates the turn, in the following section, to an evaluation of Wells’s case for a Global Minimum Income.

2. Wells’s Case for a Global Minimum Income

In Just End Poverty Now, Wells defends a Global Minimum Income as a “deliberately minimalist” response to global poverty – minimalist in the sense that its aim is to argue for the least controversial moral demand: ending extreme destitution (Wells 2019, 4). Structurally, the proposal resembles a basic income scheme: a periodic, unconditional cash payment delivered directly to residents, exclusively targeted for low-income countries (Wells 2019, 3). Wells frames the Global Minimum Income as the “least we should do” for those living in absolute poverty (Wells 2019, 1). Furthermore, given the moral urgency of ending destitution, Wells emphasizes that an advantage with a minimalist framing is that it is uniquely well-positioned to attract broad moral agreement across otherwise divergent religious, ethical, and political outlooks, thereby making it practically feasible (Wells 2019, 4).

The move towards a Global Minimum Income comes from Wells’s skepticism toward two dominant mechanisms of poverty alleviation: economic growth and traditional development aid (Wells 2019, 7-8, 11). First, with respect to growth, Wells acknowledges its historical role in lifting millions out of poverty, but emphasizes that gains from economic growth do not reliably reach the poorest (Wells 2019, 9). Wells argues that a “Global Minimum Income is a superior alternative to waiting around hoping for inclusive economic growth to finally arrive.” (Wells 2019, 10) To insist that the world’s poorest and most destitute wait for growth to catch up to them is, on Wells’s view, to accept avoidable suffering under the guise of long-term optimism (Wells 2019, 10-11).

Second, Wells argues that traditional development aid also fails as a reliable remedy for ending destitution. Despite decades of investment and economic planning, development assistance has produced uneven and mixed results, particularly for the poorest of the poor (Wells 2019, 11). Development economist Angus Deaton has named this the paradox of aid: where aid is needed, it doesn’t work, and where aid works, it isn’t needed (Deaton 2015, 268). Despite its promise, traditional aid structures are often too slow, complex, and institutionally fragile to function as a reliable guarantee against destitution.

It is against this background that Wells proposes a Global Minimum Income as an alternative that is both simpler and immediately actionable alternative to growth-led poverty reduction and traditional aid. By establishing an unconditional income floor for the world’s poorest, the proposal aims to end extreme poverty without waiting for broader structural improvements to arrive.

Viewed through the lens of republican freedom, Wells’s Global Minimum Income proposal has several compelling features.

First, by directly targeting the world’s poorest, a Global Minimum Income would, if successful, undermine one of the most pervasive sources of domination: extreme destitution. As argued above, destitution makes individuals and communities vulnerable to arbitrary power. Beyond the obvious moral case of eliminating suffering due to material deprivation, a guaranteed income floor would reduce domination by expanding the material conditions necessary for meaningful independence. Even a modest and partial income security may significantly improve bargaining power, reduce vulnerability to coercion, and enable individuals to refuse the most exploitative social and economic arrangements, such as wage slavery (Wells 2019, 13; Taylor 2018, 4; Laborde 2010, 56).

Second, the unconditional nature of cash transfers significantly reduces paternalism. Unlike many aid programs, which operate through technocratic and top-down interventions, a Global Minimum Income allows individuals to decide for themselves how resources are best used. This respect for agency aligns closely with the republican concern for non-arbitrary interference. Beneficiaries are not required to conform to donor-defined priorities, nor are they subject to externally imposed behavioral conditions in order to qualify for support. In addition, when it comes to spending money rationally, Wells refers to evidence that poor people “tend to do a better job of it than NGOs and government agencies.” (Wells 2019, 12)

Third, Wells’s proposal can also be seen as a transitional move away from domination-prone discretionary charities and donor-specific prioritized aid schemes toward a more rule-like global entitlement structure. By grounding a Global Minimum Income as a law-like standard, it would promote a system of redistribution guided by general rules rather than the ad hoc benevolence of donors. In this respect, a move towards a Global Minimum Income may serve as a bridge within aid reform discussions to transition away from domination-prone aid structures to a more robust and predictable form of global redistribution based on a robust rule of law.

With that said, it is important to emphasize that the Global Minimum Income is not presented as a replacement for all other forms of development assistance, nor as a substitute for domestic provision of basic services. Rather, it is conceived as an immediate non-discretionary mechanism for eliminating absolute poverty without waiting for economic growth or traditional aid structures to deliver such results. In this sense, the proposal resonates with broader calls for aid reform that extend beyond republican theory (Civita, Note 49).

At this stage, a critical question must be posed. Might not the institutional architecture required to implement a Global Minimum Income reintroduce domination in new forms? A central republican concern is that the institutional structure that would make a Global Minimum Income effective may also generate new relations of dependence and domination. It is these concerns that the next section will address.

3. Global Minimum Income and the Risk of Relocated Domination

From a republican perspective, the question is not only whether domination by local elites is reduced, but whether domination is instead relocated to international agencies and donor coalitions with arbitrary power. First, one notable feature of Wells’s Global Minimum Income proposal is its institutional bypass architecture. Where domestic institutions are assessed as weak, corrupt, or unreliable, Wells proposes that “an international agency acting independently of the local government” – theorized to be incorporated within the United Nations – should assume primary responsibility for the funding and disbursement of cash transfers (Wells 2019, 15). Whereas in countries with stronger institutional capacity, the UN agency would operate in partnership with domestic governments, and where feasible, responsibility for distribution could eventually be transferred fully to national authorities themselves (Wells 2019, 15).

At the outset, this architecture appears well aligned with the republican motivation to end extreme poverty. If destitution renders individuals and communities dependent, then bypassing corrupt or dysfunctional domestic institutions may indeed reduce domination. By insisting that resources reach recipients directly, a Global Minimum Income would address one of the most pervasive sources of domination: extreme material dependence.

On the other hand, the bypass move also risks generating new structures of power asymmetry, shifting power away from local institutions and toward an international agency. Importantly, despite the unconditional nature of the cash transfer of the payment scheme, certain questions are still left open. For example, someone must build the registry of recipients, define identity and eligibility, decide what counts as non-compliance or fraud, and control the switch that pauses or reinstates payments, and so on. Who sets these rules, who can contest them, and how? What safeguards prevent administrative discretion from becoming arbitrary uncontestable control?

One specific strand of this concern is expressed by Larry Temkin in Being Good in a World of Need (Temkin 2019). Relying on data by Deaton, Temkin warns that bypassing domestic governments can weaken local governance structures and that “well-intentioned interventions by aid groups can undermine the local governments’ responsiveness to their citizens’ needs, interests, and will.” (Temkin 2019, 7) From this we can understand that if basic provisions are secured externally, then citizens may no longer rely on or meaningfully engage with their own governments. Over time, this can generate a negative feedback loop in which domestic institutions worsen rather than strengthen, leading to increased dependence (and domination) in the long run.

While Temkin’s worry is directed at the NGO-heavy architecture of traditional aid and not necessarily at the Global Minimum Income proposal that Wells has in mind, it is still an important point to consider. If access to basic provision comes from an international source that bypass local institutions, it can weaken the citizen-state accountability of poor countries. The extent to which this would be the case for a Global Minimum Income scheme – and to what degree it may still be justified for the sake of ending destitution – is a complex question without an obvious answer. If anything, this underscores the significance of ensuring that any Global Minimum Income architecture must be institutionalized in ways that reduce domination dynamics to the furthest extent possible – a point I will return to in the next section.

Another strand of republican concern, however, becomes particularly salient when Wells proposes ideas such as bilateral implementation as a possible first step. Although he admits that “a global agreement would be ideal,” Wells suggests that a single wealthy state could “twin” with a poorer one – such as the Netherlands with Malawi – and roll out a direct cash transfer program independently of broader international coordination (Wells 2019, 19). While this is pragmatically attractive, from a republican perspective it exemplifies precisely the form of domination that a non-dominating redistributive scheme should avoid. If Malawi’s residents become structurally dependent on Dutch support for their basic income floor, it would expose them to the discretionary and arbitrary power of Dutch politics. If the Netherlands decided to exit the arrangement, Malawi and its residents would have no standing to contest this shift in priorities and no protection against unilateral withdrawal. In other words, Malawi would be dominated by its (benevolent) Dutch twin.

That said, this dynamic is not unique to the bilateral example above – in fact, it characterizes much of the traditional development aid system as a whole, where “about 80 percent of ODA [Oversees Development Assistance] is bilateral.” (Deaton 2013, 261) Consider the case of Nepal, where multiple donor countries pursue distinct and sometimes overlapping development priorities. In Nepal, Norway puts emphasis on gender equality, hydropower, and food security; Germany prioritizes resilient agriculture and solar energy; and Switzerland focuses on infrastructure, particularly suspension bridges connecting remote villages, to name but a few. These priorities are defensible on their own merits, and many yield tangible benefits. Yet from a republican standpoint of freedom as non-domination, the structural problem lies elsewhere. Nepal’s access to these resources do not rest on stable, rule-bound entitlements, but on the discretionary priorities of foreign governments – priorities shaped by domestic elections, the effects of aid reforms and geopolitical circumstances, to which Nepal has no standing whatsoever. At any point, funding streams may be redirected or withdrawn for reasons entirely beyond Nepal’s control and contestation.

Consider, for example, the 2025 disruption of USAID following shifts in U.S. domestic politics. From a republican perspective, two analytically distinct failures can be identified. First, the structural dependence of low-income countries to USAID was itself a form of domination, since recipients relied on the goodwill of an external agency whose priorities they could not contest. Second, the decision to shut down USAID constituted the actual exercise of that power. The consequences of the latter were obvious and tangible, but they were only made possible by the former. This is why, from a republican perspective, poverty eradication should not rest on arbitrary donor benevolence, but should take the form of rule-bound entitlements that are insulated, as far as possible, from arbitrarily shifting domestic and geopolitical priorities.

Having laid this out, it is important to emphasize that republican concerns about a Global Minimum Income should not be read as a reason to reject its implementation. If Wells is right about the moral urgency of ending destitution, republicans should resist letting ideal theory become an excuse for prolonging injustice. Still, once we take seriously the domination dynamics that have long characterized development assistance, we are better positioned to evaluate how a Global Minimum Income should be implemented in the least dominating manner possible. This is the focus of the final section.

4. The Least-Dominating Global Minimum Income

Taking stock before proceeding, the argument has so far has established three core claims. First, eradicating extreme destitution is – beyond any further moral reasons – a neo-republican priority, since poverty entrenches relations of dependence and domination across the world. Second, the Global Minimum Income’s core design – unconditional cash transfers – aligns well with the republican concern for agency and non-domination, especially insofar as it reduces exposure to arbitrary donor discretion and shifting priorities. Third, a Global Minimum Income is not domination-proof, and its institutional form matters for whether it reduces or reproduces dependency.

To address this third point, this final section compares the two implementation pathways for a Global Minimum Income found in Wells that seem most likely to be pursued by donor countries. For each, I evaluate the domination risks and sketch out design features that reduce them.

The first pathway is a multilateral model organized under a global agreement. This is Wells’s preferred direction, where an international body – possibly within the UN system – manages the overall operation of the fund (Wells 2019, 15). From a republican standpoint, the key advantage here is that a multilateral arrangement significantly reduces the domination-effect associated with bilateral aid: recipients are much less exposed to the discretionary power of any single donor state. A multilateral model also shifts the relationship from being a donor-recipient dynamic to a general and impersonal entitlement, reducing the scope for aid to function as strategic leverage.

Yet a multilateral model can generate domination in other ways. The responsible agency would hold significant power over tasks such as building recipient registries, classifying cases of fraud, and deciding when withhold or resume payments. Even if the cash transfers themselves are unconditional, the administration of the agency may still be dominating if administrative decisions are uncontestable.

To reduce these risks, a multilateral model must be institutionalized in a rule-of-law-like manner. Participating donors must treat commitments as obligations, not voluntary year-to-year pledges. Withdrawal should remain possible but highly constrained so that the scheme is resistant to political volatility and does not leave recipients at the mercy of changing geopolitical priorities. The 2025 USAID incidence should be a reminder: what makes aid domination-prone is not only withdrawal, but the standing vulnerability created by the mere capacity to arbitrarily withdraw. A multilateral design should therefore ensure exit power is constrained by binding commitments and long notice periods for withdrawal, for example by requiring a minimum 5-year withdrawal-window. Finally, equal standing must be secured for recipients. Those subject to the scheme (or their representatives) must have a meaningful role in how the scheme is governed and operated, so that they can contest that which they are being subjected to.

The second pathway is bilateral implementation. Wells notes that, absent global agreement, a single rich country could “twin” with a poorer one and roll out direct cash transfers bilaterally (Wells 2019, 19). The advantage of this route is its practicality; it can be implemented immediately, without the need to wait for a global agreement. But it is also the most domination-prone, precisely because it concentrates the power to withdraw in one donor and personalizes the relationship between funder and recipient. Even under benevolent conditions, recipients remain dependent on a relationship that can be altered or ended unilaterally as a result of electoral turnover, shifting priorities, or domestic politics. If bilateral twinning is pursued, the domination-risks must be managed carefully.

First, to reduce abrupt withdrawal, bilateral arrangements should be bound by a robust multi-year agreement (e.g. 10-20 years), overseen by a neutral and responsible third party. The bilateral scheme should not be set-up as a favour, but as a public commitment to poverty eradication.

Second, the arrangement should be deliberately de-strategized. The bilateral twinning scheme should not be used as a strategic tool by rich countries. If a donor state is serious about poverty eradication on republican terms, one option could be to commit ex ante to twinning, but only then select the recipient through a randomized allocation mechanism. This would reduce the perception that the partnership is chosen for strategic gain.

Third, bilateral twinning must ensure co-governance and equal standing. Even unconditional transfers require registries, fraud protocols, dispute resolution, audits, and oversight. If these functions are controlled unilaterally by the donor, domination persists. Bilateral schemes should therefore incorporate meaningful joint governance. Where feasible it should operate through the domestic institutions in the recipient country to avoid jeopardizing local governance legitimacy (Temkin 2019, 8).

Comparing the two alternatives through a republican lens, the multilateral model is preferable, since it dilutes donor leverage and makes assistance more rule-like. Bilateral twinning may still be justified considering non-ideal constraints, but it should be tightly fenced in to reduce dominating structures of power. Much more can be said about the republican constraints that should guide the institutional design of a Global Minimum Income. The central takeaway, however, is this: ending destitution should be done in ways that do not merely shift those in extreme poverty from one form of dependence to another, but instead secure poverty eradication on terms consistent with equal standing and non-domination. This, too, may be the “least we should do.”

Conclusion

This paper has defended a Global Minimum Income as a plausible and morally compelling response to extreme poverty when assessed through the lens of republican freedom. Drawing on Thomas Wells’s Just End Poverty Now, it has argued that a Global Minimum Income can weaken a central enabling condition of domination by reducing acute material dependence of the world’s poorest. Compared to traditional aid arrangements, direct and unconditional transfers also respect recipient agency while avoiding layers of technocratic and arbitrary donor-driven priorities.

At the same time, from a republican perspective, poverty reduction is not sufficient to secure freedom as non-domination. Even an effective redistributive mechanism can remain normatively deficient if it operates on discretionary terms, insulated from contestation, or exposed to arbitrary withdrawal. The risk is not that a Global Minimum Income fails to reduce poverty, but that it reduces poverty while relocating domination from the domestic level to international agencies, donor states, or shifting geopolitical priorities.

To address this, the paper compared two implementation pathways found in Wells’s proposal: a multilateral model and a bilateral “twinning” model. While the multilateral route is preferable in republican terms, bilateral implementation may still be justified under non-ideal circumstances, provided it is designed to minimize dependence and secure equal standing. If it’s designed properly, a Global Minimum Income can meet the moral urgency of “just ending poverty now,” while also meeting the republican demand to helping without dominating.
