**Introduction**

For much of its history, Western philosophy has treated evil as a kind of nothingness. Socrates understood it as ignorance of the good. Christian thinkers such as St. Augustine developed “privation accounts,” according to which evil is a deficiency or absence within an otherwise rational and good order. Leibniz, in his theodicy, famously argued that despite appearances, we nonetheless inhabit “the best of all possible worlds.” Across these traditions, evil is framed as a lack: a shadow, an error, or a failure of reason rather than a force in its own right.

A common and intuitive feeling associated with this view is the belief that evil can be eliminated. If moral education were improved, or if technological progress advanced, or if cultural and spiritual societies were perfected, then evil could be a thing of the past. In other words, a future utopia is possible – indeed, it waits for us – granted we organize ourselves and our communities properly, by eradicating ignorance, sin and imperfection.

This essay argues that such views rest on a fundamentally flawed metaphysical understanding of freedom. Drawing on F. W. J. Schelling’s *Philosophical Investigations into the Essence of Human Freedom* (2006), it contends that evil is not a deficiency or a lack, but a real and ontologically necessary dimension of a living reality. The central thesis is that evil is intrinsic to the very possibility and structure of freedom, and that without it we would live in what Schelling calls a “dead universe;” devoid of struggle and meaning.

If this is correct, then utopian projects – be they political, technological or moral – that aim to eradicate evil are not merely naïve but metaphysically self-defeating. I argue that by trying to socially engineer away the possibility of evil, utopian attempts necessarily contradict the conditions under which freedom, life and meaning can arise. A world without the possibility of evil would not be a perfect world; it would be a mechanistic, dead and lifeless world.

To develop this argument, the essay proceeds in three steps. First, I explain the “freedom controversy” following Kant’s philosophy, where freedom appears threatened by moral formalism. Second, I reconstruct Schelling’s ontology of freedom, according to which evil is not a mere privation but an ontologically positive possibility internal to freedom itself. Third and finally, I argue that any utopian project premised on the eradication of evil risks eroding the very conditions of freedom.

## 1\. Kant and the “Freedom Controversy” 

Before we can grasp the profundity of Schelling’s philosophy of freedom, it is necessary to first understand the problem he inherited from Immanuel Kant, which Fichte famously called the “freedom controversy.” (Smith 2020, 741) Kant’s philosophy of morality and freedom left German Idealism with a difficulty it could not ignore, which this section will make explicit.

In the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1996), Kant argues that freedom lies in autonomy and rational self-legislation: to be free is to act according to a universal law given to oneself through the capacity of reason. Freedom is not the ability to choose among competing options in the empirical world, but rather the capacity to bind one’s actions freely to the moral law through universal maxims (Kant CPR 2004, 5:31). In this way, Kant locates freedom within the very structure of rationality itself (Kant CPR 2004, 5:29-5:30). A will is free insofar as it is not determined from without – such as by inclinations or desires – but instead determines and constitutes itself through a universal law.

In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1998), Kant writes, “The will is thought as a capacity to determine itself to acting in conformity with the *representation of certain laws*. And such a capacity can be found only in rational beings.” (GMM 1998, 4:427-4:428) From this, we get the sense of Kant’s position: to fail to adhere to the moral law, which is given to us through reason, is not an expression of freedom but a kind of *in*capacity; a failure of reason to obey its own dictates. Kant reinforces this view in the *Critique of Practical Reason*,

“The moral law…proves not merely the possibility of freedom, but that it really belongs to beings who recognize this law as binding on themselves. The moral law is in fact a law of the causality of free agents and therefore of the possibility of a supersensible system of nature, just as the metaphysical law of events in the world of sense was a law of causality of the sensible system of nature…” (CPR 2004, 5:31).

As Daniel J. Smith explains in *An ethics of temptation* (2020) on his discussion of Kant’s position, Kant separates freedom from the possibility of wrongdoing: freedom is a capacity (*Vermögen*) grounded in the lawgiving of reason, whereas deviation from the moral law is not an expression of freedom but an *in*capacity (*Unvermögen*) in its exercise (Smith 2020, 735). In other words, the will is free insofar as it follows reason. When it does not, something has gone wrong. Evil, then, cannot be an expression of freedom. Rather, like other privation accounts like Socrates or St. Augustine, evil is understood as a deficiency or a mistake. A rational being who freely chooses to act irrationally appears, for Kant, to be a contradiction in terms. If a free agent could freely will evil, freedom itself would contain irrationality, thereby undermining its identification with rational self-legislation.

Already during Kant’s time, his contemporaries were quick to press this issue and a number of critical questions were raised: “What does his account of freedom as autonomy mean for theories of free will? What is the status of immoral action in practical philosophy?” (Smith 2020, 731) Following our discussion, we may add some questions of our own. If freedom is intrinsically bound to rationality, how is it possible for a free being to act irrationally? Can a rational agent freely will evil? These questions lay at the heart of what came to be known as the “freedom controversy.” (Smith 2020, 732) The core concern was about the relationship between freedom and evil: does freedom include the genuine capacity to choose evil, or is evil precisely the failure of freedom?

Carl Schmid, one of Kant’s contemporaries, took the implications of Kant’s philosophy to its logical extreme, by adopting a position of “intelligible fatalism.” (Smith 2020, 733) Schmid, following Kant, “saw no reason to think that the intelligible causality of freedom would be any less deterministic than the mechanistic causality of nature,” and in doing so “re-introduced fatalist determinism at the higher \[moral\] level.” (Smith 2020, 733-734) Schmid thought that that if freedom is nothing but the dependence of the will on reason, then a free will cannot do otherwise than follow the moral law.

Another contemporary of Kant, who found this “mechanism of morals” completely unacceptable was Karl Leonard Reinhold (Smith 2020, 733). Reinhold argued that Kant’s conception of freedom was incomplete because it could not account for evil. If freedom cannot genuinely choose evil, in what sense is it freedom at all? To make sense of moral experience as it is actually lived, Reinhold insisted on a “second, distinct kind of freedom which is our capacity to follow *or not follow* this practical law that we have prescribed to ourselves through reason.” (Smith 2020, 733) This was a “fundamental faculty” (grundvermögen), that, according to Reinhold, is “well-captured by the common concept of free will.” (Smith 2020, 733) Reinhold insisted that without this real possibility of choosing evil, moral concepts like praise and blame would lose their meaning, because a will that by necessity follows reason would be similar to a kind of rational compulsion; it resembles a “spiritual automaton” rather than a morally responsible agent confronted with moral choice (Smith 2020, 733). To account for the empirical reality that we sometimes *freely* choose evil, Reinhold concluded that freedom must also include the capacity to freely act *against* reason.

According to Smith, “there is a long-standing debate about how to understand Kant’s position with respect to Reinhold on this point” especially considering Kant’s “essay on radical evil, which has been an unending source of controversy in the literature,” which this essay will not attempt to resolve (Smith 2020, 733). However, noteworthy for our purposes here, is that Kant’s concept of radical evil is an attempt by Kant to reconcile the empirical reality of evil with a rational grounding of freedom. To do so, he postulates that human beings can indeed willfully invert the proper moral order by subordinating the universal moral law to the self-interested will. Still, Kant concedes, “though we still cannot comprehend how this is possible.” (Smith 2020, 733). So even if Kant postulates radical evil as something real (rather than a mere lack), he fails to ground evil within reason.

It is this ungroundedness of evil that motivates the turn to Schelling’s philosophy. To ground evil, Schelling moves beyond the domain of morality and into ontology. In doing so, Schelling radically reconfigures the relationship between freedom and evil while building on the tensions brought forth by the freedom controversy.

## 2\. Schelling’s Ontological Turn: Freedom and the Dark Ground

To account for a real, positive concept of evil that is not only postulated but grounded in reality, Schelling recognized the need to move beyond the confines of Kantian moral philosophy. In his 1809 *Philosophical Investigations into the Essence of Human Freedom,* Schelling sets out to do exactly that. He shifts the philosophical inquiry from the moral realm into a metaphysical investigation into the fundamental structure of being itself. Through his metaphysics of being, Schelling makes an attempt to provide an adequate account of freedom in the face of the reality of evil.

At the heart of Schelling’s metaphysics lies an irreducible polarity – one that cannot simply be eliminated – like on privation accounts. Schelling argues that being itself is structured by a tension between two co-eternal principles: a dark principle and a principle of light. These are called, respectively, Ground (Grund) and Existence (Existenz). To Schelling, the tension, struggle and dynamic interplay between these two principles constitute the condition for life, and as we will come to see, allow for a a conception of freedom that can accommodate evil.

The Ground is what Schelling calls “nature – in God, a being indeed inseparable, yet still distinct, from him.” (Schelling 2006, 27). It is not God as revealed, intelligible or self-conscious, but rather the obscure basis from which revelation itself must come from. The Ground is blind, contracting, and inward turning. It is a principle of yearning and non-rational drive with the destructive potentiality if it becomes its own center (*centrum*) (Schelling 2006, 30). Schelling describes the Ground as eternally anarchical and the “incomprehensible base of reality in things, the indivisible remainder, that which with the greatest extortion cannot be resolved in the understanding but rather remains eternally in the ground.” (Schelling 2006, 29) If this description of the Ground is true, then we can already start to imagine the implications this will have for utopian projects that strive to “perfect” humanity.

Opposite to the dark Ground is Existence – the principle of light, expansion and unity. Schelling identifies it with *logos*, the “Word” and the self-revelatory and intelligible nature of being (Schelling 2006, 30). If the Ground is the dark and creative yearning of becoming, Existence is the principle that organizes the yearning into form and meaning. It is through Existence that being becomes manifest to itself, that inward striving becomes outward order.

An important feature of these two principles is that one does not precede the other. Schelling writes that, “In the circle out of which everything becomes, it is no contradiction that that through which the One is generated may itself be in turn begotten by it. Here there is no first and last because all things mutually presuppose each other.” (Schelling 2006, 28) What this means is that Existence becomes actual only from the depth and resistance with the Ground, which in turn supplies the force and struggle for becoming. Likewise, without Existence, the Ground would collapse into a blind and meaningless inwardness. A chaotic drive without form or direction. Being or reality, therefore, is only actualized *through* the tension between Ground and Existence; it is from the eternal collision of the principle of darkness and the principle of light that the universe comes to be.

This is one of, if not *the*, central points of Schelling’s ontology: that the polarity between the dark and light principle is not a defect to be overcome. Indeed, the very tension between the two is what makes life *alive.* Schelling exerts considerable efforts in his attempt to argue against the classical conception of God as a pure, static and perfect actuality (*actus purissimus*), stating that such a notion of God would be nothing more than a “god of the dead” (Schelling 2006, 26). It is here Schelling criticizes “the entire new European philosophy since its beginning (with Descartes)” for constructing “empty and abstract” systems of God and Nature that lack the organic and dynamic force to “constitute a living whole.” (Schelling 2006, 26) Without this inner differentiation and incomprehensible, anarchic dark Ground, life would seize to be a living organism. Being is dynamic, personal and internally restless; it requires opposition, resistance, and struggle. He famously states, “where there is no struggle, there is no life.” (Schelling 2006, 62)

When Schelling makes these claims, he is not only talking about the human condition of life, but the condition for all life. Still, humanity occupies a unique position in Schelling’s ontology, for we occupy the most crystallized and intensified tension between the dark and light principles. Whereas in God, the principles of Ground and Existence are always inseverable and indeed “serves only the eternal joy of overcoming,” while in nature, the dark principle remains blind and unconscious, and never rises to the level of autonomy (Schelling 2006, 62). But in the human being, the unprecedented has occurred: the two principles have become severable. Schelling writes, “the same unity that is inseverable in God must therefore be severable in man – and this is the possibility of good and evil.” (Schelling 2006, 33)

It is this severability that constitute the ontological root of genuine freedom. Freedom, then, is the possibility for good and evil; it is human being’s confrontation of its severability of the Ground and Existence. This is what makes human beings ontologically special, as compared to all other creatures. Schelling writes, “this raising of the deepest *centrum* into light occurs in none of the creatures visible to us other than man. In man there is the whole power of the dark principle and at the same time the whole strength of the light. In him there is the deepest abyss and the loftiest sky…” (Schelling 2006, 32) With this we can now explain how evil becomes possible. Evil, on Schelling’s account, is the conscious possibility to “reverse” the proper relation between the dark Ground and the principle of light – in which the particular will perverts its position relative to the whole. Instead of relating one’s will to the whole, it seeks to conform the whole into its own subjective image. Evil is, properly understood, neither ignorance nor weakness. It is a real and even freely *willed* act; a metaphysical rebellion of being against itself, expressed through the human being. This, I take it, is the true inescapable tragedy of the human condition.

Two concrete examples can help us elucidate what Schelling is pointing at. The first is an analogy attributed to Franz von Baader, of evil as a disease (Schelling 2006, 35). Disease is not necessarily the absence of health. Disease can also be a parasitic *vitality*, with a life and will of its own, not really weak and aimless but rather something more terrifying: it can be a coherent, purposeful but false life, structured around an “indignant host of desires and appetites.” (Schelling 2006, 34) Likewise with evil. It is not a nothingness. It is a life severed from the duality of the two principles that has taken on a life of its own, like a parasite that has reverted the order of the organism that gives it life.

A second example appears in the animated series *Arcane* (2021) – a modern myth – where the character Jinx represents what Schellingian evil looks like. Jinx is a deeply traumatized girl whose early experiences in life fracture her relation to the world around her. Rather than integrating this suffering into a higher unity, guided by the light principle, her wounded selfhood becomes the perverted organizing center of her existence. Following Jinx’s path, it is obvious that evil is not a moral emptiness or a kind of incapacity to apply reason correctly, but in fact it is an intense, creative and destructive form of life. She illustrates perfectly that evil is not a mere deficiency, but rather a severed life that can be internally coherent but fundamentally distorted – and very dangerous.

These two examples make vivid Schelling’s central insight that evil is not merely a deficiency to be corrected for or an ignorance to be educated away. Evil is a positive ontological perversion that is nevertheless necessary for the existence of freedom; without it, reality would collapse into a dead, lifeless, and static order. The next section explores the implications of this insight for utopian projects that seek to eradicate evil in its entirety.

## 3\. The Metaphysical Mistake of Utopianism 

To preface, not all utopian projects are the same, and this paper will not attempt to cover all their varieties. The claim I advance here is more fundamentally concerned about the *underlying logic* of utopian thinking. Utopianism, broadly speaking, is understood for the purpose of this paper as the belief in the perfectibility of human beings and their environment. As such, whether it manifests as techno-optimism, political or ideological perfectionism, or moral-social engineering, it is inspired by the same underlying conviction that human life can be perfected, if only we could eliminate the evil latent in mankind. At its core, therefore, utopianism treats struggle itself as a symptom of the existence of problems that can be done away with, rather than as conditions for life and freedom.

However, from Schelling, we learn that a world without struggle would not be morally praiseworthy – it would be static, sterile and dead. *Arcane* (2021) makes this painfully vivid in the epic clash between the two inventors and friends Jayce and Viktor. Jayce represents the paradigmatic Schellingian human: a being who experiences the tragic yet redeeming reality of freedom. He has a flawed nature, and using Schelling’s vocabulary, we can say that his dark and light principles are disunited. The dark principle in him – which manifests through his reckless ambition and desire for recognition – has serious consequences. His inventions lead to massive destruction and conflict. Nonetheless, despite the wrongs committed on his account, it is through this very struggle – through his mistakes – that Jayce earns the possibility of genuine goodness and redemption. Like a properly lived life, he takes real moral risks – some which succeed and others that fail. Through his struggle in life, he exercises the freedom of what it means to be human.

Opposed to him stands Viktor, a sensitive and physically fragile genius who experiences the vulnerability and tragedy of the human condition first-hand. Through his compassion for mankind he attempts to engineer pain and suffering away entirely. His desire comes from a noble place, one shared by many. He wishes to eliminate life’s misery by removing the possibility for evil that causes it. While his intent is admirable, in abolishing the possibility for suffering, he also removes the conditions for genuine freedom and moral agency. He almost succeeds – until Jayce stops him – such that every living being are transformed to an enlightened but mechanistic being, compelled to the fatalistic “mechanism of morals” foreshadowed by Schmid. Unless one sides with Viktor, this should serve as a powerful warning to the risks of utopian thinking that wishes to engineer away our short-comings.

A contemporary manifestation of this utopian impulse can be found in forms of techno-optimism and moral bioenhancement, when taken to their logical extremes. Consider, for example, the proposals advanced in *Unfit for the Future? Human Nature, Scientific Progress, and the Need for Moral Enhancement* by Julian Savulescu and Ingmar Persson, who argue for the use of biotechnology and pharmaceuticals to reduce aggression, bias, and selfishness in human beings (Persson and Savulesco 2011, 487-490). A similar sentiment dominates discussions of AI alignment, for example “safety-by-design” digital architectures (Floridi et al. 2018, 689-707). Moral failure here is treated as a technical problem to be eliminated through biological improvements or system design rather than as a constitutive and necessary feature of human freedom. The underlying assumption is the same: if harmful options can be eliminated – engineered away – then society will become morally superior.

Some nuance should be stressed. This is not an argument against the important efforts being made to prevent potentially catastrophic outcomes resulting from moral failure in complex systems. The claim advanced here is more fundamental. As our actions and lives are progressively mediated by digital environments that structure our decision-making processes, a critical question must be asked: If negative outcomes are engineered away to the point that the possibility of failure is eliminated altogether, might the disappearance of our capacity to commit wrongs mean not the achievement of moral progress, but rather the gradual forfeiture of freedom itself? Returning to Carl Schmid’s “intelligible fatalism,” are we not creating a kind of intelligible fatalism – with silicon? What may appear as ethical progress may in actuality be, similar to Viktor’s laudable intention, the slow erasure of freedom, life and meaning.

Finally, in the Christian narrative of Genesis, life in the Garden of Eden is peaceful, pleasurable, and free from suffering; it is envisioned as a kind of heaven for humans. Only when Eve eats from the forbidden fruit from the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil does sin enter the world. This act is traditionally interpreted as the Fall of Man and a great tragedy. Yet – if we take Schelling seriously, might we not reinterpret this moment as the true and spectacular ascent of Man? By eating the forbidden fruit – by severing the principles of dark and light that are eternally united in God – humanity assumes an ontological position that is both tragic and sublime: it becomes capable of freedom. Unless we would like to live in a static and mechanistic “utopia”, we may be grateful that – despite the tragedy and suffering – we have the opportunity to live free, dynamic and self-constitutive lives in which we are morally and existentially responsible beings.

Conclusion

This essay has challenged a persistent idea in the history of thought, that evil is a lack or a mistake that can be eliminated. From Socratic ignorance or Augustinian privation, evil has been framed as something reason and progress can dissolve. Yet this way of thinking rests on an understanding of evil that Kant and the “freedom controversy” exposed as untenable. Freedom understood as rational self-legislative autonomy could not accommodate evil, but the empirical world shows that evil cannot be ignored. Schelling’s ontological turn breaks with this impasse. By grounding freedom not in rationality but in the severability of the principle of Ground and Existence, we understand evil neither as ignorance nor weakness but as a real and freely willed possibility constitutive to freedom. Freedom is only possible where the potential for good and evil exists as real alternatives.

This insight has serious consequences for utopian thinking, whether technological, political or moral engineering. If the possibility of evil is necessary for genuine freedom, then the task is not to imagine a world without evil, but to shape a future that can live with it without attempting to eradicate it. Schelling’s ontology is thus a reminder and a warning to all utopian projects, that a living universe can never be made entirely safe without ceasing to be free. Evil is – for better or worse – the price we pay for freedom.
