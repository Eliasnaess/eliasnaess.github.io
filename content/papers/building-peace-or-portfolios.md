Despite being a small country with a population of less than six million, Norway is internationally recognized as a serious actor in world politics. In 2011, US President Obama observed that Norway “punches above its weight” on matters of international peace and security (Carvalho and Lie 2014). This reputation is rooted in Norway’s century-long history as a peace nation (Leira 2013), but is also supported by one of the world’s most generous aid budgets. In 2025, Norway provided USD 5.7 billion in official development assistance, equivalent to 1.03 percent of GNI, making it the only DAC country above one percent that year and well above the UN-recommended 0.7 percent norm (OECD 2026; Norwegian MFA 2026).

A second aspect of Norway’s presence in the global political economy is its ownership of the world's largest sovereign wealth fund, the Government Pension Fund Global (hereafter the Fund). The Fund is currently valued at USD 2.2 trillion, equivalent to roughly 1.5 percent of all listed companies globally (NBIM 2025). It is set up as a savings mechanism to safeguard wealth for future generations of Norwegians, but its rapid growth has given Norway a structural presence in the global political economy that far exceeds its economic and military power. Norwegian political scientist Ulf Sverdrup described in 2016 the Fund as the “gorilla in the room” of Norwegian foreign policy, pointing to its reality as structurally enormous yet largely absent from public foreign-policy discourse (Sverdrup 2016).

These two dimensions appear to operate under different logics. Norway’s foreign policy and development efforts are political and normative. Whereas Norway’s role as a global investor is market-driven and return-seeking. This forms the background for the paper’s research question: *To what extent can Norway’s roles as peace nation and global investor be reconciled within the framework of embedded liberalism?*

This paper argues, within Ruggie’s embedded liberalism framework, that Norway represents the clearest contemporary example of a state that has embedded markets at the domestic level and attempted to extend this embedding internationally. From this perspective, Norway's dual roles are mutually reinforcing: its century-long peace-nation identity (Leira 2013) institutionalized the Fund’s ethical architecture, while the Fund supports Norway’s active engagement to the multilateral, rules-based order of its foreign policy. However, the rapid growth of the Fund and its increasing fiscal importance for the Norwegian welfare state, has structurally positioned Norway as a systems-maintaining state (Neumann 2011). The paper evaluates whether the institutional architecture of the Fund and Norway’s active role as a norm entrepreneur state are sufficient to address the deeper disembedding dynamics that take place under global capitalism.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 sets out the theoretical framework of embedded liberalism and situates Norway as a state that has embedded markets domestically and a long history toward extending liberal values internationally. Section 3 discusses sovereign wealth fund literature and the “Norwegian model” of the Fund as the institutional vehicle through which this extension is attempted. Section 4 provides a critical reading, by first identifying the structural limits of the Fund’s ethical architecture and then finally assessing the tension of Norway’s dual role in the global political economy. Section 5 concludes.

> **2.1 The Embedded-Liberal Compromise and Its Limits**

Embedded liberalism refers to the global economic system that emerged after World War II, which was a compromise between international economic openness and domestic social protections. After the economic crises of the interwar period and the destruction that followed, it became necessary for national economies to, on the one hand, participate in free trade, while on the other hand, not lose the ability to protect their populations from economic disruption. As John Ruggie (1982) writes, “the essence of embedded liberalism…is to devise a form of multilateralism that is compatible with the requirements of domestic stability.” (Ruggie, 399) To achieve this, a compromise was made. As states liberalized their economies through free trade and multilateralism at the international level, they introduced social protections and regulations domestically to “cushion the domestic economy from external disruptions.” (Ruggie, 405) This ensured that markets would remain politically and socially embedded within national societies rather than operate according to the dangerous disembedded laissez-faire liberalism that preceded it.

However, as Ruggie notes, “the compromise of embedded liberalism has never been fully extended to the developing countries.” (Ruggie, 415) Essentially, the liberal-embedded compromise was particularly well suited to industrialized states that already had the socio-political institutions necessary to implement social cushioning. Many developing countries, seeking to take part in the same benefits and prospects of global trade, also opened their economies to global capitalism. In the developing world the effect of liberalization was different. From the 1970s and 1980s, many developing economies adopted structural adjustment programs (SAPs) under the guidance of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and World Bank, with the goal of promoting liberalization, privatization, fiscal discipline, and integration into global markets. These reforms and policies were later associated with what Williamson (1990) called the “Washington Consensus,” and have since been subject to extensive criticism in the development literature for its, at best, mixed results (Williamson 1990; Pogge 2011; Chang 2003).

One strand of this criticism is captured by Chang’s (2003) “Kicking Away the Ladder: The ‘Real’ History of Free Trade”. His claim is that the initial industrialized states developed with protectionist policies and strategic state support for key industrial sectors, and only later opened up their markets to free trade (Chang 2003). Developing economies, on the other hand, were encouraged to compete in the global market without a comparable historical policy space that enabled the global North to industrialize. As a result, many developing countries struggled to protect infant industries, build a solid industrial base, and thereby successfully construct the domestic social bargain that made liberalization politically feasible in the industrialized global North. On this reading, embedded liberalism is the implementation of a global asymmetry where we find embedded markets in the industrialized North, and disembedded markets in large parts of the Global South. Some critics make the case that the current global economic order benefits rich countries, at the expense of violating the human rights of the global poor (Pogge 2011).

> **2.2 Norway as an Embedded-Liberal Peace Nation**

We now turn to Norway as the paradigmatic case of an embedded-liberal state. Having opened its economy to global markets, Norway also developed one of the most extensive welfare states in modern history. The discovery of oil in the late 1960s marked a historic turning point for Norway. How politicians managed the newly-found resource is an excellent example of the embedded-liberal compromise. Firstly, Norwegian policymakers had paid close attention to the experiences of other oil-rich economies and the macroeconomic dangers of rapid resource wealth. In particular, they sought to avoid “Dutch disease,” the phenomenon whereby a resource boom (typically petroleum) causes a sharp currency appreciation and weakens non-resource reliant industrial sectors in the rest of the economy (Øvald and Tranøy 2022). Drawing on those lessons, Norway established the Fund in 1990 as a mechanism for saving and reinvesting petroleum revenues. The logic of the Fund, which can be articulated as an embedded-liberal compromise, is to shield the domestic economy from the volatility of oil revenues and to convert finite petroleum revenues into long-term holdings to secure the welfare of future Norwegian generations (NBIM 2025).

What makes Norway particularly interesting within the framework of embedded liberalism is its long-rooted history and foreign policy as a self-identified peace nation that strives to extend liberal values internationally (Leira 2013). As Leira documents, long before the postwar bargain of embedded liberalism, dating back to the 1890s, Norway’s foreign policy agenda was explicitly identity-driven rather than merely interest driven (Leira 2013). This identity later evolved in the 1990s into *engasjementspolitikk* (politics of engagement), in which Norway’s self-image as a “humanitarian great power” has justified its active participation in matters of international diplomacy, peace mediation, development efforts and security (de Carvalho and Lie 2014). This is reflected in official Norwegian state documents from 2003 which notes “the picture of Norway as a moral and humanitarian superpower has become a new national symbol, in line with other symbols which shape Norwegian identity” (NOU 2003/19: 51, cited in Leira). Stating the same point differently, historian Rolf Tamnes claimed that, “Norway saves the world, thus Norway exists.” (Tamnes 1997, cited in Leira 2013) The identity as an important peacebuilding nation continues to be the dominant narrative reflected in official norwegian foreign policy. This is reflected in the latest foreign policy white paper, which states that “Over several decades, Norway has built up cutting-edge expertise and international political capital through diplomatic craftsmanship to mitigate and resolve conflicts.” (Meld. St. 30 2024-2025)

> **2.3 Norway as a Systems-Maintaining State**

Notwithstanding Norway’s long-rooted self-understanding as a peace-building nation, from a realist lens of international relations Norway’s position can be interpreted from systems-maintenance theory. According to Neumann’s (2011) theory of systems-maintaining diplomacy, small and medium powers whose security and prosperity depend on the continued stability of the existing international order have a strong interest in maintaining that order (Neumann 2011). As a small nation, Norway does not possess the power to re-design the world order, neither would it be in its interest to do so as it benefits from maintaining the status quo. From this lens, Norway’s strong commitment to global stability through development aid, multilateralism, and insistence to international law (Meld. St. 30 2024-2025) can be understood beyond the Norwegian narrative of peace. Within Neumann’s framework, Norway’s *engasjementspolitikk* can be read as a means for maintaining the global economic system from which its own embedded economy benefits.

In light of the Fund’s unprecedented growth over the last decade, this framing becomes increasingly prescient. In recent years, approximately 20 percent of Norway’s annual state budget is financed by the Fund (NOU 2022:12: 9; Øvald and Tranøy 2022). In other words, a substantial share of Norway’s fiscal capacity is now directly dependent on returns generated through Norway’s participation in the global financial market. This complicates the relationship between Norway’s normative commitments as a peace-nation and the motive behind its foreign policy objectives. Norway may genuinely be, as Leira (2013) documents, a nation of a peace-oriented people that strives to promote justice, development, and human rights internationally. Yet these efforts also help stabilize and legitimate the global economic system that it is in Norway’s direct self-interest to preserve. From this perspective, aid and peace diplomacy can be read as mechanisms that “soften” the dislocations of global capitalism while nonetheless maintaining the very system through which those dislocations are produced.

Yet from Norway’s own point of view, this does not necessarily need to collapse into cynicism. Its normative commitments and liberal internationalism long predate its structural dependence on the global financial system (Leira 2013). Moreover, the empirical record shows that global economic integration has contributed substantially to the overall reduction of absolute poverty, even if this progress has unfolded unevenly (NOU 2022:12). The Fund and Norwegian foreign policy can therefore be understood as mutually reinforcing mechanisms within an unequal world. As the Fund has grown in size and become increasingly central to the financing of Norway’s welfare state, it has also helped sustain Norway’s international engagement. Norway’s aid budget has consistently increased alongside the size of its economy, reaching its highest level of official development assistance in 2025 at approximately USD 5.7 billion, equivalent to 1.03 percent of GNI (OECD 2026). On this reading, Norway’s investor-state role does not contradict its liberal internationalist peace-nation identity; it helps finance it.

Taking stock, by returning to Ruggie’s framework of embedded liberalism and to critiques of its uneven extension to the developing world (Chang 2003; Pogge 2011), a central tension can be identified in Norway’s position. On the one hand, Norway seeks to project its normative values internationally through peacebuilding, development assistance, and its support for multilateralism. On the other hand, it is structurally dependent on maintaining the current system of global capitalism, whose benefits remain unevenly distributed and whose disembedding effects are felt most sharply in parts of the Global South. The next section therefore turns to the sovereign wealth fund literature in order to examine the GPFG as the core institutional vehicle where Norway’s normative ambitions and structural dependence on global finance most clearly converge and experience tension.

> **3.1 Sovereign Wealth Funds in Global Finance**

Before examining Norway’s Fund in detail, it is useful to situate it within the broader category of sovereign wealth funds (SWFs). SWFs are state-owned investment instruments that manage public assets separately from a country’s ordinary fiscal budget and foreign exchange reserves (IWG 2008; Beck and Fidora 2008). Many of the largest SWFs have been established by resource-rich states seeking to transform finite natural-resource revenues, such as petroleum revenue, into long-term financial wealth for future generations. However, as Caner and Grennes observe, “lack of transparency and incomplete data make it impossible to perform an objective analysis of most SWFs” (Caner and Grennes 2010: 601). Generally, they are state-owned public institutions that operate under a market-driven logic that closely resemble large private institutional investors. Because of this, SWFs occupy an ambiguous position in the global economy.

The rapid growth of SWFs has generated considerable debate about their role and effects in global financial markets. On the one hand, SWFs are generally regarded as stabilizing actors. Their long investment horizons and absence of short-term performace pressure make them a predictable source of liquidity, particularly in times of market stress (Beck and Fidora 2008). On the other hand, SWFs have also been treated with a degree of suspicion because they blur the line between state power and market investment (Beck and Fidora 2008; Stückelberger et al. 2016). The three main concerns are that SWFs may pursue politically motivated investments, that large divestments could amplify market volatility, or that state-owned capital could be used as instruments of geopolitical influence rather than purely commercial return (Beck and Fidora 2008; Balding 2012). These concerns have at times generated protectionist responses, as “the claim by the USA, Canada and Europe that SWFs could be a threat for national interest is based on fear that SWF home countries might obtain too much power over their domestic affairs.” (Stückelberger et al. 2016)

In response to these concerns, the development of international governance frameworks for SWFs was initiated, coordinated by the IMF. In 2008, twenty-three countries with established SWFs collaborated, including Norway, to draft the Generally Accepted Principles and Practices for SWFs, commonly known as the Santiago Principles (IWG 2008). These voluntary principles were primarily designed to promote transparency, accountability, and independence from direct political interference. The Santiago Principles remain the most widely recognized framework for legitimate SWF conduct. Consistent with its *engasjementspolitikk,* Norway played an active role in this development, advocating for standards consistent with global best practice.

With this background, the next section will show that Norway’s operation of the Fund does not merely comply with the voluntary standards; it scores at the very top of every major governance index and actively works to promote adherence by other actors at the international level.

> **3.2 The Fund as Ethical Benchmark and Norm Entrepreneur**

This section examines the Fund’s institutional architecture and Norway's role as a norm entrepreneur in global SWF governance, as a deliberate expression of its broader normative and structural commitments to the global economic system. Among the worlds SWFs, Norway’s Fund is consistently featured as the ethical benchmark. In Truman’s (2007) assessment of SWF governance, the GPFG scored 92 out of 100 on governance best practices, received the maximum score on the Linaburg-Maduell Transparency Index and is ranked among the most compliant funds with the Santiago Principles (Clark, Dixon and Monk 2013: 78).

As the world's largest SWF, the GPFG’s decisions carry weight well beyond Norwegian borders. If the fund excludes a company, then other large institutional investors tend to reassess their own exposure to that firm (Nguyen et al. 2024). Aware of this role, Norway functions in this capacity as a norm entrepreneur state and leverages its financial power to promote standards that subsequently shape the behavior of others (Finnemore and Sikkink 1998). Thus, by demonstrating that financial power can be exercised in a transparent and compliant way while still generating competitive returns, the Norwegian model has raised the bar for what responsible SWF conduct can look like.

Furthermore, the intricate institutional architecture of the Fund, most notably with the establishment of the *Council on Ethics* in 2004, reflects Norway’s attempt to minimize the unethical effects its financial footprint may have internationally. As the Fund continued to expand around the turn of the millennium, “the idea of the Norwegian state ‘speculating with’ state revenue in the international stock market was an unfamiliar concept,” and a growing number of civil society actors grew uneasy with the prospect of profiting from companies engaged in unethical conduct (Øvald and Tranøy 2022). As a result and on official recommendation by the Graver Commission, the establishment of an independent Council on Ethics was made, which parliament adopted unanimously (NOU 2003:22). The Council’s purpose is to screen out companies deemed ethically unacceptable, based on product-based criteria (weapons, tobacco, and thermal coal, etc.) and conduct-based criteria (human rights violations, environmental damage, gross corruption, etc.). Assessments are submitted as recommendations to Norges Bank, which operates the Fund, and in nearly all instances follows the Council's advice on exclusion or observation. Unlike most other SWFs, this architecture makes the Fund remarkably democratically responsive. A recent example that demonstrates its responsiveness is the Fund’s decision in 2025, because of political pressure, to exclude companies complicit in activities in the occupied Palestinian territories, on the grounds of serious violations of international law (Council on Ethics 2025). It is important to note here that the NOU 2022:12 commission acknowledges the difficulty of calibrating this framework, noting that authorities should “take care to avoid any excessive gap between expectations for what can be achieved through the ethical framework, on the one part, and what can realistically be achieved, on the other part” (NOU 2022:12: 43)

To take stock, Norway's role in international governance frameworks and the institutional architecture of the Fund – especially through the Council on Ethics – represent a committed attempt to extend the embedded-liberal compromise beyond its borders. Its active role as a norm entrepreneur state in shaping international governance standards is an expression of its normative commitment to a rules-based and cooperative international order. The question however, is whether this architecture is sufficient to reconcile Norway’s investor-state role with its peacebuilding identity. Section 4 takes up this question by introducing critical financialization literature, which suggests that the Fund’s ethical framework reaches certain structural limits.

> **4.1 The Limits of Ethical Investment**

The systems-maintaining reading developed above raises a sharp question. If Norway’s interest in maintaining the global economic order is not only normative but increasingly structural, then the relevant issue is not merely whether the GPFG avoids investment in clearly unethical companies. It is whether responsible investment at the company level is sufficient to address the wider disembedding effects of global finance. The critical finance literature suggests that it is not a straightforward answer.

In *Risky Returns: The Implications of Financialization in the Food System*, Clapp and Isakson (2018) show how financial logics can reorganize entire sectors around the narrow objectives of shareholder return and capital accumulation. In their study of the agrifood sector, they argue that this process “exacerbates the existing imbalances of power and wealth in the food system; it increases economic and ecological vulnerabilities within agrifood systems; and it has evolved in ways that impede and dampen collective demands for change and resistance” (Clapp and Isakson 2018: 437). Critically, none of this requires any individual company to be acting illegally or in violation of an ethical code. The effects emerge at the level of the sector. A similar argument applies in global value chains, as Bair and Werner (2011) show that populations may be displaced from circuits of economic value not through any firm’s individual misconduct, but through structural processes of disarticulation that occur between firms and across sectors. A fund that screens companies for misconduct has no mechanism to assess whether the sectors in which it is invested are themselves organized in ways that produce these types of outcomes.

This points to a structural limit with the Norwegian model. Even as the Council on Ethics screens individual companies for clearly defined violations such as serious human rights abuses, environmental damage, corruption, much of the disembedding operates at the sectoral level. Apart from a few product-based exclusions, such as the tobacco, the framework is primarly designed to assess companies rather than sectors. This means that a commercial company working in agriculture may therefore satisfy the Council’s ethical criteria while still participating in the broader financialization of food systems that Clapp and Isakson describe. The Norwegian model thus shows a limit as to how far ethical investment can be institutionalized within global finance. While the Fund may act responsibly at the company level, the overall financialization effects of its sectoral exposure may still contribute to outcomes that works against Norway’s aspiration to extend the liberal-embedded compromise beyond its borders, or at least to reduce its complicity in facilitating disembedded markets abroad.

**4.2 Reconsiling the Peace Nation and the Investor State**

How, then, should Norway’s dual role as a committed peace nation and investor state be assessed? This paper has argued that, in important respects, the two roles are mutually reinforcing. Norway’s value-based foreign policy provided the cultural and institutional conditions for the Fund’s ethically responsive architecture, while at the same time, the Fund supports Norway’s commitment to peacebuilding and to the multilateral, cooperative order that its foreign policy has long defended. Through its *engasjementspolitikk* and its position as the world’s largest SWF, Norway acts as a norm entrepreneur, using financial power to promote governance standards that other states and investors take seriously. Within the constraints of the existing global economic system, the Fund therefore represents one of the most developed attempts by any state to extend embedded-liberal values internationally.

Yet Norway’s position can also be read more critically. When Ruggie’s account of embedded liberalism and its incomplete extension to developing countries is read alongside Neumann’s systems-maintaining diplomacy and the sectoral disembedding effects identified in the financialization literature, Norway appears not only as a norm entrepreneur, but also as a state with a structural interest in sustaining the global economic order from which it benefits. Its aid, peace diplomacy, and ethical investment strategy may therefore help stabilize and legitimize a system on which Norway’s own welfare state increasingly depends. Seen in this light, Norway’s actions can be read as maintaining, rather than challenging, an order that continues to produce uneven distributional outcomes, especially in the disembedded markets in the Global South.

There may not be a clean resolution to this tension. If the systems-maintaining thesis holds, Norway has a direct interest in preserving a stable global economic order with open and predictable markets. Given its normative aspirations, however, its most coherent response is would therefore be to deepen its role as a norm entrepreneur. It should continue to lead and extend best practices in responsible investment, while engaging more explicitly with the sectoral financialization dynamics that its current ethical framework does not fully capture. This may constrain short- to medium-term returns and invite further complexity for its investment strategy, but it may be a path worth exploring. As NOU 2022:12 notes, countries have “experienced increasing internal inequality, and this may be a source of social and political instability.” (NOU 2022:12: 24) Thus, if the disembedding effects of global financial markets become significant enough, this may prompt rising powers to construct a rival global economic order from which Norway is not guaranteed to benefit. Norway’s task should therefore not simply be to invest responsibly within a disembedded global financial order, but to help re-embed that order itself, especially where global capitalism most prominently reproduce vulnerability, inequality, and domination.

> **5. Conclusion**

Norway’s unique position demonstrates both the moral aspirations and the structural tensions of embedded liberalism in the twenty-first century. This paper has argued, within Ruggie’s framework, that Norway represents one of the clearest contemporary examples of a state that has embedded markets domestically while attempting to extend this logic internationally. Its century-long peace-nation identity helped shape the Fund’s ethical architecture. At the same time, the Fund’s growing fiscal significance means that Norway’s support for the multilateral rules-based international order increasingly structural, making Norway a systems-maintaining state. Yet the paper has also identified a structural limit. The Fund’s ethical architecture operates primarily at the level of the company, but the disembedding effects that the critical finance literature documents in agrifood systems and global value chains operate at the level of the sector. Norway’s dual role in the global political economy is therefore complex and represents an ongoing attempt to reconcile the moral ambitions of liberal internationalism with the structural realities of global capitalism.
